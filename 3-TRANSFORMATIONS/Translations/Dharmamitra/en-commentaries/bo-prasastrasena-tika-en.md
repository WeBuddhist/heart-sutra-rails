# Praśāstrasena's Extensive Commentary on the Noble Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom ^0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^0-1]]

In the language of India: Ārya-prajñāpāramitā-hṛdaya-ṭīkā. ^0-1

## Explanation of this Sūtra ^1-0

### The Designation of Wisdom ^1-1-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-1]]

In the language of Tibet: Ārya-prajñāpāramitā-hṛdaya-ṭīkā. Homage to Mañjuśrī-kumārabhūta and to the noble Avalokiteśvara.
To explain this sūtra, it shall be clarified through ten topics: the name of prajñā, the introductory context, the meditative absorption, the raising of the topic, the engagement with prajñā, the characteristics of prajñā, the sphere of activity of prajñā, the qualities of prajñā, the fruit of prajñā, and the dhāraṇī of prajñā.
This is the name of prajñā: because it is not possible to cognize an entity without a name, the name is stated in order to focus upon the entity. Therefore, it is called the "noble prajñāpāramitā-hṛdaya."
Regarding that "wisdom" (prajñā), it is said to be of this kind: mundane wisdom, supramundane wisdom, and unsurpassable wisdom.
Mundane wisdom is distorted, for it perceives the impermanent as permanent, the impure as pure, the painful as pleasant, and the selfless as a self.
Supramundane wisdom is the wisdom of the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas who know that there is no self in the person; they know that conditioned things are impermanent, that conditioned things are painful, that there is no self in the person, and they know the peace of nirvāṇa.
Unsurpassable wisdom is the tathāgata’s knowledge that there is no self in persons or phenomena; it is the knowledge of signlessness, wishlessness, and emptiness. This is what is meant by unsurpassable wisdom.
"Noble" (ārya) means that by this wisdom, one is far removed from sorrow and suffering.
"Prajñā" means the supreme wisdom of the mundane and the supramundane; it is wisdom that is unsurpassable.
"Pāramitā" means that birth, death, and suffering are the near shore, while nirvāṇa is the far shore. Sentient beings, driven by the desires of cyclic existence, are in between. By using this wisdom as a boat or a raft, one is brought to the shore of nirvāṇa, and thus it is called "pāramitā."
"Hṛdaya" means that because all the sūtras of the hundred-thousand-verse chapter of the prajñāpāramitā are gathered within this, it is called the "heart." Alternatively, because it is proclaimed as the supreme among all that the tathāgata has taught, specifically this very ultimate prajñāpāramitā, it is called the "heart." ^1-1

### Introduction ^1-2-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-2]]

This is the introductory framing. If it were not taught, one would not know where, to whom, or what was being taught; therefore, the introductory framing is stated in order to teach the location and the audience to whom it was taught. The phrase "Thus have I heard" refers to the meanings that follow below, and by this, superimposition and denial are abandoned. The phrase "by me" indicates that it was heard directly; it shows that the Dharma was heard directly, but that the meaning was not yet realized. The word "heard" means that it was grasped by the consciousness of the ear faculty capable of listening. The phrase "at one time" means that it was heard on a single occasion, either in the morning or in the evening. The term "Bhagavān" is used because he has abandoned the afflictions that are to be abandoned through seeing and meditation. Where was it taught? Because of that, "Rājagṛha" is stated, for that specific city is called Rājagṛha. Since that city is extremely vast and there is no fixed place where he was residing, "Gṛdhrakūṭa mountain" is stated, because that mountain is naturally shaped like a vulture. Because all afflictions are vanquished, he is a "bhikṣu." Because he cannot be divided by opponents, he is part of the "saṅgha," and "great" is used because of the greatness of their number and qualities. Because they establish themselves in unsurpassable enlightenment, they are "bodhisattvas." Because they establish all sentient beings on the level of non-abiding nirvāṇa, they are "mahāsattvas." "Residing" should be understood as residing in terms of deportment. By these, it is shown when, by whom, where, and to whom it was taught, clarifying the objects examined by realization. When was it taught? It was taught at one time. By whom? By the Bhagavān, and by this, the perfection of the teacher is shown. Where? At Rājagṛha, on Gṛdhrakūṭa mountain, and by this, the perfection of the location is shown. To whom? To the bhikṣus and the bodhisattvas, and by this, the perfection of the retinue is shown. ^1-2

### Meditative Attainment ^1-3-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-3]]

This refers to meditative absorption; if one does not enter into meditative absorption, one does not clearly realize the objects of investigation, so meditative absorption is mentioned in order to clearly realize the objects of investigation. The phrase "at that time" refers to the time when the Blessed One was dwelling in Rājagṛha, and it should be connected with entering into meditative absorption and so forth. If one asks, "What is the meditative absorption that is called 'Profound Illumination'?" it is for this reason that the "profound Dharma discourse" is mentioned. In this context, phenomena are so called because they grasp their own and general characteristics. The "discourse" on those phenomena refers to the collections of phenomena, namely the five aggregates, the elements, and the sense spheres. "Profound" refers to the meaning of emptiness by nature, because there is no arising and no ceasing. If one enters into meditative absorption, one realizes that phenomena such as the aggregates are without arising and without ceasing; therefore, the name of that meditative absorption is called "Profound Illumination Dharma discourse," where "illumination" refers to realization. "Entered into" means to continuously place the mind one-pointedly upon meditative absorption. ^1-3

### Explanation of the Introductory Precepts ^1-4-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-4]]

This refers to the teaching of the introductory discourse; if the introduction is not established, the words would become confused and incoherent, so the introduction is mentioned in order to make the words coherent and not confused. Furthermore, the phrase "at that time" refers to the very time when the Blessed One was dwelling in Rājagṛha, and it should be connected with the statement, "The noble Avalokiteśvara looks upon the five aggregates as empty by nature." "Bodhi" (enlightenment) refers to the essence of mind, suchness, and the ultimate limit. One who strives and exerts oneself to attain that is a bodhisattva. One is an "ārya" (noble one) because one has cast off the two obscurations far away. Regarding "Avalokiteśvara," because he looks upon all sentient beings with a compassion that is in the manner of non-apprehension, arising from the three types of compassion, he is Avalokiteśvara. Because he has attained self-mastery in order to dispel the suffering of sentient beings, he is "īśvara" (lord). Regarding "prajñā" (wisdom), there are three types of wisdom: it is the "sword of wisdom" because it cuts the knot of the five aggregates; it is also called the "vajra of wisdom" because it destroys the heap of suffering; and it is also called the "lamp of wisdom" because it dispels the darkness of ignorance. In short, wisdom that realizes all phenomena as empty through non-conceptual wisdom in every respect is called prajñā. Because one crosses over to the other shore by means of that wisdom, it is "pāramitā" (perfection). There are three types of pāramitā: the worldly pāramitā, the supramundane pāramitā, and the supramundane ultimate pāramitā. Among these, the worldly pāramitā is the abandonment of the suffering of the three realms of the path. The supramundane pāramitā is the attainment of the peace of partial nirvāṇa after having abandoned the cycle of birth and death. The supramundane ultimate pāramitā is the attainment of non-abiding nirvāṇa, where, because the three realms are like a dream, there is no desire even for nirvāṇa, and because sentient beings are like an illusion, there is no hope even for the fruit of buddhahood, and because all phenomena are by nature in nirvāṇa, one attains the ultimate pāramitā. If one asks what that prajñāpāramitā is, it is for this reason that "profound practice" is mentioned. "Profound" means without arising and without ceasing. "Practice" refers to the meaning; that which is without arising and without ceasing is called prajñāpāramitā. "Conceptualization" is the apprehension of the meaning of that which is without arising and without ceasing. ^1-4

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-5]]

It is not only that one focuses on the meaning of being without birth and without cessation, but one also views the five aggregates, such as form, as empty. Regarding this, there are two types of emptiness: the emptiness of unconditioned space, and the emptiness of the ultimate, which is the self-cognizing awareness of the noble ones’ wisdom, free from subject and object. The meaning is that one views the five aggregates as empty, being free from subject and object. There are three types of views: the views of ordinary beings and non-Buddhists, the views of Shravakas and Pratyekabuddhas, and the views of Bodhisattvas and Tathāgatas. Regarding this, ordinary beings and non-Buddhists view the five aggregates as a life-force, a self, or a person. Shravakas and Pratyekabuddhas view the five aggregates as arising and as suffering. Bodhisattvas and Tathāgatas view and behold the five aggregates as empty by their very nature. ^1-5

### Engagement with Wisdom ^1-5-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-6]]

This is an entry into the perfection of wisdom; because the method of entry is not understood, the words of inquiry are presented. What are those? They are the words beginning with "Then." Why did the noble Śāriputra not ask another bodhisattva, but instead asked the bodhisattva, the great Avalokiteśvara? Regarding this, it is said: when this perfection of wisdom was being taught, that bodhisattva was the foremost among the countless hundreds of thousands of the assembly; therefore, through the power of the Buddha, the noble Śāriputra asked him the questions that follow in order to dispel his own doubts and those of others. The words "Then" mean "after what has been taught." The words "through the power of the Buddha" mean "through the blessings of the Tathāgatas." Regarding the name "Śāriputra," it is so called because it was derived and designated from the name of his mother, Śārikā. The words "spoke these words" mean that he asked the questions that follow. ^1-6

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-7]]

The words "whosoever" mean "whoever it may be." Regarding the "perfection of wisdom," wisdom is twofold: the wisdom that knows the conventional and the wisdom that knows the ultimate. Among these, the wisdom that knows the conventional is knowing all phenomena to be like illusions, mirages, and dreams. The wisdom that knows the ultimate is knowing all phenomena to be inexpressible and unthinkable, like the nature of space; because that wisdom leads to the attainment of non-abiding nirvāṇa, it is called the "perfection of wisdom." In essence, the profound meaning of the absence of birth and cessation is called the perfection of wisdom. "Practice" refers to the person, the bodhisattva. "Practiced" refers to the dharma, the perfection of wisdom. The words "how should one train?" mean: how should one enter into the meaning of the perfection of wisdom through the methods of listening, reflecting, and meditating? In two ways, one should train in the four types of practice. The four types of practice are: practicing the ultimate through non-conceptual wisdom; practicing the factors of enlightenment without defilements; practicing the ripening of sentient beings without attachment; and practicing the ripening of the Buddha-dharma without grasping. The words "how should one train?" mean: how should one practice these four types of practice? ^1-7

### The Characteristics of Wisdom ^1-6-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-8]]

This is the characteristic of prajñā; if one does not know the characteristic, one cannot grasp the meaning, and therefore it is taught by means of words that respond to [the phrase] "bodhisattva mahāsattva" and so forth. The phrase "said to Śāriputra" means that which follows, [which is] the meaning of the words "said thus." ^1-8

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-9]]

The phrase "should be examined in this way" means that one should practice the prajñāpāramitā in this way. If one asks, "How should one train?" it is for that reason. ^1-9

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-10]]

The phrase "one should view those five aggregates as empty of intrinsic nature" is stated. The five aggregates are form, feeling, perception, formative factors, and consciousness. Regarding the phrase "empty of intrinsic nature," there are five types of emptiness: emptiness of what did not exist before, emptiness of what has ceased to exist, emptiness of what is absolutely non-existent, emptiness of one thing not being in another, and emptiness of intrinsic nature. Among these, the absence of yogurt in milk is the emptiness of what did not exist before. The absence of milk in yogurt is the emptiness of what has ceased to exist. The absence of horns on the lower part of a rabbit's head is the emptiness of what is absolutely non-existent. The absence of a horse in a cow is the emptiness of one thing not being in another. The fact that all phenomena lack intrinsic nature is the emptiness of intrinsic nature. Thus, among the five types of emptiness, the meaning is that one should examine the five aggregates as being empty by way of the emptiness of intrinsic nature. ^1-10

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-11]]

Regarding the phrase "form is empty," "form" refers to earth, water, fire, and wind. "Empty" means that because the ultimate dharmadhātu is characterized by emptiness, those forms are emptiness. That characteristic of emptiness is non-dual, beyond counting and calculation, free from views of self and what belongs to self, and devoid of grasping and what is grasped; thus, it is the characteristic of non-dual tathatā. Since all phenomena are included within the characteristic of emptiness, the four elements—earth, water, fire, and wind—are also without characteristics, without substantiality, without a self, and without a master. It is not that they are broken down into atoms and then become emptiness; rather, because they are empty by intrinsic nature, it is said that "form is empty." That which is form empty by intrinsic nature is the ultimate emptiness itself; because there is no ultimate emptiness separate from form empty by intrinsic nature, it is also said that "emptiness is form." This means that the ultimate emptiness is the nature of the emptiness of form. If one asks what is the evidence that the emptiness of form is ultimate emptiness, the *Ārya-Akṣayamati-nirdeśa* states: "Even though the dharmadhātu of the wisdom that enters the dharmadhātu of the bodhisattva is the elements of earth, water, fire, and wind, the dharmadhātu is not characterized by hardness, moisture, heat, or motion. The dharmadhātu and all phenomena are the same." If one asks why, it is explained that they are the same because they are the same in being emptiness. Therefore, one should understand that the emptiness of form is ultimate emptiness.
Regarding the phrase "form is empty" in two ways, there are three types of form: fully conceptualized form, mentally constructed form, and form as the nature of phenomena. Among these, what ordinary, childish beings conceptualize as the hard characteristic of earth and so forth is fully conceptualized form. Form that is the object of consciousness associated with meaning is mentally constructed form. The characteristic of tathatā, which is free from fully conceptualized form and mentally constructed form, is form as the nature of phenomena. Because that form as the nature of phenomena is devoid of fully conceptualized form and mentally constructed form, it is said that "form is empty." If one thinks that the emptiness of form as the nature of phenomena exists separately from mentally constructed form and fully conceptualized form, it is stated that "emptiness is form," meaning that the emptiness of form as the nature of phenomena and the mentally constructed and fully conceptualized forms are identical in characteristic.
Regarding the phrase "that which is form is emptiness; emptiness is form; there is no emptiness separate from form," the emptiness of the characteristic of form and the ultimate emptiness are not different; because they are one, it is said that there is no emptiness separate from form. If one asks why they are not different, it is because they are not different in the characteristic of emptiness, which is the essence of being free from additions, free from subtractions, and having abandoned the two extremes. Regarding the phrase "that which is form is empty," this means that whatever is the characteristic of form is also the characteristic of emptiness. The phrase "that which is emptiness is form" means that whatever is the characteristic of emptiness is also the characteristic of form.
By this, the following is taught: base and childish sentient beings, from beginningless time, revolve within the cycle of birth and death in the five realms. They are familiar with the five aggregates and accustomed to the eighteen dhātus; because they are attached and cling to them, grasping them as substantial entities and masses, the Tathāgata taught that they are empty by intrinsic nature. If, when the characteristic of form is destroyed into emptiness, one grasps at the antidote—emptiness—as existing separately, one is attached to the one-sided peace of the nirvāṇa of the Śrāvakas. Thus, the phrase "that which is form is emptiness" is taught as the antidote to falling into the edge of cyclic existence due to attachment to form. The phrase "that which is emptiness is form" is taught as the antidote to falling into the edge of nirvāṇa, where, after realizing that there is no self in the person, the Śrāvaka destroys form into emptiness and subsequently a sign of emptiness arises. ^1-11

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-12]]

Since form and emptiness are non-existent, they are to be abandoned. In this regard, if emptiness is taught in order to abandon the grasping of form as a sign, then because a mind that grasps emptiness as a sign arises, both emptiness and form are subsequently abandoned. A sign is that which, by being focused upon, becomes an obstruction to realizing reality itself; thus, grasping emptiness as a substantial entity is also a sign. To illustrate this: suppose a person with blurred vision is traveling as a guest and there are thorns and pits on the right side of the road, and there are chasms and precipices on the left side of the road. If a person with unimpaired vision says, "There are thorns and pits," one falls into the chasms and precipices; if they say, "There are chasms and precipices," one falls into the thorns and pits. By showing the middle path—the state of well-being that is unobstructed by anything—one is made to reach one's destination. This example is similar: "those with blurred vision" refers to ordinary beings who are obscured by the obscuration of afflictions, and to śrāvakas who are obscured by the obscuration of knowables. ^1-12

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-13]]

"Thorns and pits" refers to individuals who are attached to signs such as form, and thus fall into the abyss of cyclic existence. "Chasms and precipices" refers to śrāvakas who are attached to nirvāṇa, and thus fall into the abyss of emptiness. The "person with vision" is the Tathāgata, who, because his eye of wisdom is clear, sees that form is empty by its very nature; thus, because birth and death are like an illusion, he does not abandon cyclic existence. Because the three realms are like a dream, he does not seek the qualities of nirvāṇa either; rather, by entering the middle path—the path of signlessness, wishlessness, and emptiness—he has reached the state of non-abiding nirvāṇa. Therefore, to show that grasping at signs is a precipice and thus a fault, one should not grasp at either existence or non-existence as a sign. ^1-13

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-14]]

Regarding the statement "feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness are empty," it is said as follows: In the preceding discussion on viewing the five aggregates as empty, the emptiness of form was mentioned, and by explaining that form is empty and stating the phrasing that shows emptiness and form are inseparable, this becomes sixfold when applied to the three types of explanation. The three types of explanation are: first, explaining that they are empty by nature and thus one; second, applying them to the three types of imputed phenomena and explaining that they are of one taste; and third, abandoning the two extremes—the ordinary person of cyclic existence who dwells in the extreme of substantial existence, and the śrāvaka who dwells in the extreme of empty nirvāṇa—and establishing the entry into the middle path. Just as it becomes sixfold by applying the phrase "form is empty, emptiness is form" to each of the three, this method is also explained for the five or four aggregates. Just as the mutual emptiness was explained through five aspects, the remaining aggregates—feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness—should also be explained through the five aspects of mutual emptiness. These phenomena are the continuum of one's own mind, and the mind, as the characteristic of emptiness devoid of form, ripens from latent tendencies and depends upon that aggregate of form. This is like an empty vessel, in that it depends upon the vessel. If that vessel is destroyed, there is no basis for dependence, and it is inseparable from great emptiness. It is similar to this: because the aggregate of form is examined as empty, that aggregate of mind also has no location, and is inseparable from the ultimate dharmadhātu. If one asks what makes it evident that the five aggregates are empty by nature, it is explained in the *Akṣayamatinirdeśa*: The aggregate of form is like a lump of foam, unable to withstand being grasped or cut. The aggregate of feeling is like a bubble of water, impermanent because it is momentary. The aggregate of perception is like a mirage, because it grasps at what is mistaken due to the thirst of craving. The aggregate of mental formations is like a plantain tree, having no essence when dismantled. The aggregate of consciousness is like a dream, because it grasps at what is illusory. Therefore, the five aggregates are not a self, not an individual, not a sentient being, not a life-force, not a nourisher, and not a person. The nature of the five aggregates is not like this; the nature of the five aggregates is like this: they are empty of self and what belongs to self. They are unborn. They have not arisen. They are non-existent. They are the expanse of space. They are unconditioned. They are by nature nirvāṇa. Thus, by knowing the four great external elements as empty, form is empty. By knowing the four internal mental [aggregates] as empty, it is called "emptiness of emptiness," and by abandoning the sign of form and emptiness being one, and being free from the two of the grasper and the grasped, it is called the liberated body. ^1-14

### The Domain of Analytical Wisdom ^1-7-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-15]]

This is the object of experience of prajñā; because if one does not know the object of experience, it is not possible to meditate, it is explained in order to cause the yogi to meditate. "Śāriputra, in this way" refers to what has been explained above. Regarding the phrase "all phenomena are emptiness," "all phenomena" refers to those based upon the five skandhas, and it should be understood as referring to the five faculties, the objects, the dhātus, the āyatanas, dependent origination, and so forth. Because one realizes those five skandhas to be empty, one should know their branches also to be of the nature of emptiness. For example, because one realizes the main body to be empty, one should know the limbs and so forth to be empty by implication. ^1-15

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-16]]

Regarding the phrase "without defining characteristics," because all phenomena are beyond marks and beyond marks, they are called "without defining characteristics." ^1-16

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-17]]

Regarding the phrase "unborn, unceasing," that which did not exist before but later comes to exist is called "born." That which existed before but later ceases to exist is called "ceasing." This essential nature of the Buddha, the ultimate reality of the dharmadhātu, which is emptiness, has no beginning, and therefore no end can be found. For that reason, it is called "unborn" and "unceasing." ^1-17

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-18]]

Even when sentient beings are wandering within the five realms of existence, because there is no becoming stained in the essential nature of the Buddha, it is called "not pure." Even when one has fully and perfectly awakened to unsurpassed, perfect, and complete enlightenment, because there is no purity that is superior to the essential nature of the Buddha, it is called "not impure." ^1-18

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-19]]

Even when one has taken the body of an ant or a beetle, because there is no becoming smaller in the essential nature of the Buddha, it is called "not decreasing." ^1-19

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-20]]

Even when it has become the dharmakāya, because there is no increase, it is called "not increasing." Why is that? Because it transcends thought and expression, and is beyond the reach of estimation. ^1-20

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-21]]

21 In two ways, because the dharmadhātu is not born from karma and afflictions, it is "unborn." Because it is unborn, it is not subject to destruction, and thus it is "unceasing." ^1-21

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-22]]

22 Because the dharmadhātu is naturally pure, it is called "not pure" because there is no [further] purity [to be attained]. Although it is naturally pure, it is not the case that adventitious afflictions cannot be purified, and therefore it is [also] called "pure." ^1-22

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-23]]

24 Because the aspect of defilement is abandoned, there is no decrease in the dharmadhātu, and thus it is "not decreasing." ^1-23

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-24]]

When the aspect of purification increases, there is no increase in the dharmadhātu, and thus it is "not increasing." Previously, the five aggregates were analyzed separately and shown to be empty; yet, if the mind still conceptualizes the five empty aggregates as signs, then because there is no division within emptiness, it is not appropriate to designate them by names such as "form" and so forth. This is said in order to eliminate the signs of conceptualizing names. ^1-24

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-25]]

In that way, emptiness is not form. ^1-25

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-26]]

It states "not feeling" and so forth. The five aggregates are empty by their very nature; because they are beyond names and designations and possess the characteristic of being indivisible, they do not exist as the signs designated as "form" and so forth, and therefore it is "not form." It says "not feeling," which means there is no form and there is no feeling. Alternatively, it means that in emptiness, one does not express the name "form," nor does one express the name "feeling." ^1-26

It is not the eye. ^1-27

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-28]]

It is not the ear. ^1-28

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-29]]

It is not the nose. ^1-29

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-30]]

It is not the tongue. ^1-30

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-31]]

It is not the body. ^1-31

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-32]]

Regarding the phrase "it is not the mind," it is said: this refers to the six sense faculties. Since the six sense faculties are dependent upon the five aggregates, if the five aggregates do not exist, then because the six sense faculties are not other than the five aggregates, they are said to be non-existent. ^1-32

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-33]]

It is not form. ^1-33

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-34]]

It is not sound. ^1-34

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-35]]

It is not smell. ^1-35

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-36]]

It is not taste. ^1-36

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-37]]

It is not touch. ^1-37

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-38]]

Regarding the phrase "there are no phenomena," it is said: these are the six objects. Because of the power of ignorance, karmic latencies ripen, and the mind, being deluded, grasps at them as existing as the result of conditioned things. However, in ultimate reality, because they are empty by their own nature, it is said that they do not exist. ^1-38

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-39]]

Regarding the phrase "there is no eye element, up to and including there is no mental consciousness element," it is said: these are the eighteen elements. Because the six internal faculties do not exist, there is no support for the six consciousnesses. ^1-39

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-40]]

Because the six external objects do not exist, there is no basis for the six consciousnesses. Since they do not arise in isolation, the six consciousnesses also do not exist; therefore, it is said that the eighteen elements also do not exist. ^1-40

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-41]]

There is no ignorance. ^1-41

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-42]]

There is no exhaustion of ignorance. There is no aging and death. ^1-42

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-43]]

Regarding the phrase "there is no exhaustion of aging and death," it is said: This is the sphere of activity for those who have entered the vehicle of the Pratyekabuddhas. The teaching from ignorance up to aging and death is stated in brief; one should view it as: conditioned by ignorance are formative factors, and then consciousness, name and form, the six sense spheres, contact, feeling, craving, grasping, existence, birth, and aging and death. What is called "ignorance" arises from the view of a self. Grasping at a self arises from the non-existence of an entity; therefore, ignorance also has no entity. For example, when a lamp appears in a house that has been in darkness for a long time, the darkness vanishes in an instant, and the entity of that non-existence does not appear either. Likewise, when the thick darkness of ignorance, which has been with sentient beings since beginningless time, is examined by the lamp of wisdom, it vanishes in an instant. The entity of that non-existence does not appear either. What is called "awareness" is merely designated in relation to ignorance; because ignorance does not exist, the name "awareness" is also not established, and therefore it is said that there is no ignorance and no exhaustion of ignorance. Because the entity of ignorance itself does not exist, there is no ignorance. Because it does not appear as an entity of non-existence, it is said that there is also no exhaustion. ^1-43

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-44]]

Regarding the phrase "there is no suffering, origination, cessation, and path," it is said: This is the sphere of activity for those who have entered the vehicle of the Śrāvakas; these are called the four truths. There are three types of truths: worldly, supramundane, and the ultimate supramundane truth. Among these, the worldly truth is knowing the five aggregates as origination and as the suffering of aging and death. The supramundane truth is knowing them as suffering, origination, cessation, and the path. The ultimate supramundane noble truth is knowing that there is no birth in the five aggregates; this is knowing the noble truth of suffering. Knowing that which destroys existence is knowing the noble truth of origination. Knowing that ignorance and latent tendencies are non-existent by their own nature is knowing the noble truth of cessation. Not superimposing anything upon all things because they are far removed in the equality of phenomena is called knowing the noble truth of the path. Thus, because the truths themselves are also non-existent by their own nature in the ultimate sense, it is said that there is no [suffering, origination, cessation, and path]. ^1-44

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-45]]

If one asks, "What is the evidence that the truths are non-existent by their own nature?" it is explained in the *Ārya-akṣayamati-nirdeśa-sūtra*: "What is a bodhisattva who is skilled in the truths? This aggregate is suffering; one who realizes the characteristic of suffering as it truly is, as emptiness, is called the noble truth of suffering. Whatever causes of craving and causes of views constitute the five aggregates is the origin; one who does not grasp those causes of craving and causes of views, and does not superimpose anything upon them, is called the noble truth of the origin. One who knows that the aggregates do not arise from the past limit, do not shift to the future limit, and do not abide in the present, is called the noble truth of cessation. One who is established in non-dual wisdom and knows the four truths as emptiness is explained as the noble truth of the path." ^1-45

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-46]]

Regarding the phrase "there is no wisdom, no attainment, and also no non-attainment," it is said: This is the sphere of activity for one who has entered the Great Vehicle of the bodhisattvas. That which is called the non-dual wisdom—the wisdom that has abandoned the two obscurations, the obscuration of the afflictions and the obscuration of the knowable—is, in the ultimate sense, not wisdom; therefore, it is said that there is no such wisdom. ^1-46

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-47]]

"Attainment" refers to attaining something that was previously non-existent. If there were attainment, there would also be emptiness, and if there were a result, it would be subject to destruction; therefore, the essence of Buddhahood, which abides equally in all sentient beings, is empty from the beginning, and in the end, there is no attainment. The mention of the sequence of the ten stages is merely the gradual purification of the latencies of ignorance within the *ālaya-vijñāna*. Once the latencies of ignorance are purified, in the *dharmadhātu*—which is like the mirror-like wisdom of a Buddha—there is no designation of "attainment" or "non-attainment," and therefore there is no attainment. ^1-47

### The Qualities of the Perfection of Wisdom ^1-8-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-48]]

"And there is no non-attainment," it is said. Therefore, the *Saptashatikā Prajñāpāramitā* also states: "Not practicing in any signs is practicing in the *prajñāpāramitā*." It is taught that "attaining nothing is attaining unsurpassed awakening." This is a quality of the *prajñāpāramitā*. Although the yogi's unsurpassed awakening is of the nature of emptiness, it is not the case that there is no fruit of the *prajñāpāramitā*; rather, its qualities are explained in order to generate enthusiasm by saying that it exists. Regarding the phrase, "Therefore, Śāriputra, because a bodhisattva has no attainment, they rely on and dwell in the *prajñāpāramitā*," it is said: "Therefore" refers to what has been explained above. "Having no attainment" means that there is no attainment of the fruit of unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening. ^1-48

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-49]]

"Relying on and dwelling in the *prajñāpāramitā*" means not dwelling in any signs whatsoever. ^1-49

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-50]]

"There is no obscuration of the mind" means that all internal habitual tendencies and external signs have been abandoned and are non-existent. Regarding "there is no fear," because there is no such obscuration of the mind, there is no fear of birth in *saṃsāra* or the lower realms, nor is there fear regarding the meaning of the profound Dharma being without production or cessation. ^1-50

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-51]]

Regarding "having completely transcended inverted views," the *ālaya-vijñāna* perceiving the non-existent external objects of the habitual tendencies of ignorance as having various forms is called "inverted." By realizing that there is no self in persons or phenomena, and by abandoning the obscurations of the afflictions and the obscurations of knowledge, one has transcended inverted views. ^1-51

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-52]]

Regarding "having reached *nirvāṇa*," ignorance is the basis of all afflictions. It is taught that because it also becomes the cause of *saṃsāra* within the twelve links of dependent origination, in the end, it results in the gathering of nothing but a heap of suffering. If one knows that there is no ignorance, then all suffering and all afflictions also become non-existent. Therefore, it is said that one has reached *nirvāṇa*. ^1-52

### The Fruit of the Perfection of Wisdom ^1-9-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-53]]

This is the fruit of the *prajñāpāramitā*; because if there were no fruit, the practice would also be meaningless, the fruit is explained. ^1-53

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-54]]

Regarding "all the Buddhas who reside throughout the three times rely upon the *prajñāpāramitā*," it is said: the times are the distinct past, future, and present times. "Reside" refers to those who have gone through the three times. "Buddha" means one who has obtained the eye of wisdom from the defilements of the *saṃsāra* of the three realms; in essence, having become the *dharmakāya*, that which is endowed with countless perfect qualities—such as wisdom, primordial wisdom, meditative stabilization, the major marks, the minor signs, and the unshared qualities—is called a Buddha. "Relying upon the *prajñāpāramitā*" means abiding in the state where all signs are non-existent. Since all the Buddhas of the three times equally arise from the *prajñāpāramitā*, this *prajñāpāramitā* is called the mother of all Buddhas. ^1-54

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-55]]

Regarding "attained manifest, complete, and perfect awakening to unsurpassed, true, and complete enlightenment," it is said: "unsurpassed" means having transcended the three realms. "True and complete" means the ultimate end of the unerring *tathatā* (suchness). "Awakening" means the *dharmadhātu* of *tathatā*. "Attained enlightenment" means that by practicing the *prajñāpāramitā* in a manner free from conceptualization, one manifests buddhahood, which is not different from the Buddhas of the past and is equal in qualities. ^1-55

### Dhāraṇī of Wisdom ^1-10-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-56]]

This is the *dhāraṇī* of *prajñā*, mentioned for the sake of not falling away. Therefore, regarding the phrase "true and not false," it is said: because there is no speech through words and no aspiration, it is true in terms of speech. Because there is no bodily conduct, there are no signs, and thus it is true in terms of body. Because there is no mental conceptualization, it is true in terms of mind, arising from emptiness. In this way, it does not contradict the three gates of liberation of the noble ones, and because it accords with the meaning of non-conceptualization—the gate of entering simultaneously into the ultimate reality by having eliminated all mental signs of the *prajñāpāramitā*—it is true and not false. It is the *mantra* of the *prajñāpāramitā* because it eliminates all latent tendencies of inner consciousness. ^1-56

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-57]]

It is the *mantra* of great knowledge because it knows and eliminates all outer signs by their essential nature. ^1-57

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-58]]

It is the *mantra* of the unsurpassed because it eliminates all signs of both outer and inner. ^1-58

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-59]]

It is the *mantra* equal to the unequaled because it accomplishes the fruit of buddhahood. ^1-59

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-60]]

It is the *mantra* that pacifies all suffering because it abandons all lower realms and performs the benefit of sentient beings. ^1-60

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-61]]

Regarding the phrase "should be known," it is said: the meaning is that this *prajñāpāramitā* should be known as the cause that brings one to buddhahood. ^1-61

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-62]]

The mantra of the *prajñāpāramitā* is proclaimed: ^1-62

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-63]]

*Tadyathā gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā.* ^1-63

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-64]]

This *prajñāpāramitā* mantra becomes a cause for both mundane and supramundane merit. Mundane merit is that which, in effect, is capable of protecting one so that one is not harmed by defilements, *māras*, and obstacles. Supramundane merit is that which, by relying upon this *dhāraṇī*, becomes a cause for *prajñā* and *jñāna*. ^1-64

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-65]]

Regarding the phrase "Then the Blessed One arose from that samādhi," it is said: because no one other than the Tathāgata is capable of explaining the meaning of the *prajñāpāramitā* and remaining in meditative equipoise, the fact that the noble Avalokiteśvara explained it and Śāriputra listened is due to the blessing of that samādhi; thus, because the activities of explaining and listening were performed, he arose from that samādhi. ^1-65

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-66]]

Having looked upon the purity of the cause and the purity of the result, the Blessed One gave his approval to the noble Avalokiteśvara, saying, "Excellent!" Having looked upon the purity of the cause and the result, the Buddha gave his approval, saying, "Excellent!" ^1-66

And the gods, humans, and so forth, ^1-67

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-68]]

They rejoiced and took great delight in what the Blessed One had spoken. ^1-68
