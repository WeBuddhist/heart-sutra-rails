# Tāranātha's Wondrous Word Commentary on the Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom Sūtra ^0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^0-1]]

The Marvelous Word-for-Word Commentary on the Heart Sūtra. A Word-for-Word Commentary on the Heart Sūtra. ^0-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^0-2]]

Namo Buddhāya. To state briefly the meaning of the Heart Sūtra of the Prajñāpāramitā: although the term "Prajñāpāramitā" applies to many meanings, its actual and primary meaning is the non-dual wisdom that is the abiding nature of all knowable things, the Sugatagarbha. This sūtra teaches three primary points: the characteristic of being free from conceptual elaboration, the characteristic of being immutable, and the characteristic of being all-encompassing, which are the nature of that Prajñāpāramitā; and because its words are extremely concise, it is called the "Heart." How does it teach them? By phrases such as "no form," it teaches the freedom from conceptual elaboration; by phrases such as "no arising," it teaches the immutable; and by phrases such as "form is emptiness," it teaches the all-encompassing. ^0-2

## 6. Establishing the Nature of the Perfection of Wisdom ^9-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-1]]

"Form is emptiness," and so forth: if we apply the two truths together, in this state where the form aggregate appears to ordinary childish beings, the form aggregate does not exist, because that form is nothing more than delusory appearance and is empty of its own essence. Therefore, form is empty, being non-existent and not present from the perspective of its own nature. ^9-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-2]]

The reality of emptiness that remains in the place of form is itself the actual emptiness; this is the emptiness that is true, existent, and real. Regarding the reality of emptiness that exists in the mode of being, although it appears as the form aggregate to ordinary childish beings, it is nothing more than delusory appearance and does not exist by its own essence. ^9-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-3]]

If we take form as the basis of the property, the *dharmadhātu* of emptiness does not exist separately from it, because that very place where the appearance of form arises is the *dharmadhātu*, and because the basis of the property—the form aggregate itself—has never existed. ^9-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-4]]

Because it is not possible to establish the *dharmatā* of the form aggregate as something separate and distinct, and because that awareness of *dharmatā* is also free from conceptual elaboration, one does not grasp the characteristics of form and its counterpart as being separate. If one takes the *dharmadhātu* of emptiness itself as the basis of the property, then form does not exist separately from it, because even though the *dharmatā* remains, the form aggregate does not exist, and also because of the aforementioned reasons. For example, if a person with a bile disorder looks at a conch shell, they do not see it as a white, coiled mass, but see it as a yellow, coiled mass. Although they see it, that yellow, coiled appearance is nothing more than a delusory appearance and has never existed from the beginning, whereas the white is existent. Therefore, considering the way in which the appearance of the yellow arises and the white are not two separate bases, but are one, it is said: "What you see as yellow is none other than the conch. The conch is that yellow which you see. Apart from that yellow which you see, there is no conch. Apart from the conch, that yellow which you see is not established." This is very much in accord with the meaning. According to those who assert *rang stong* (self-emptiness), because they hold that emptiness pervades the ultimate, the form aggregate would end up being the ultimate truth; thus, while it is valid to say that "emptiness is not form, and it is not other than form," the statement "form is emptiness; emptiness is form" is not acceptable. In this way, this is the second statement that clearly demonstrates the great *gzhan stong* (other-emptiness) Madhyamaka. Explaining it in this way is to show it as it is clearly stated in the negation phrases of this sūtra, but if explained according to the hidden meaning, it is purely an affirmation: "Whatever is the *dharmatā* of ultimate form, that is the primordial wisdom of emptiness; whatever is the primordial wisdom of emptiness, that appears as the *dharmatā* of form." By teaching this, even if one understands that the two share a common basis, in order to dispel the doubt that "it is possible that the primordial wisdom of emptiness is not the ultimate form, and it is possible that the ultimate form is not the primordial wisdom of emptiness," [the sūtra says] "form is also..." and so forth. In this context, "emptiness" does not mean mere emptiness, but the primordial wisdom of the emptiness of the two. Although the ultimate form aggregate is not the form aggregate, that ultimate aspect of the form aggregate within the all-encompassing *dharmadhātu* is called the *dharmatā* form aggregate or the ultimate form aggregate. ^9-4

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-5]]

The same logic applies to the subsequent items. Likewise, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness are empty. Emptiness is the range from feeling to consciousness. Emptiness is not other than the range from feeling to consciousness, and the range from feeling to consciousness is not other than emptiness. Thus, it should be applied. ^9-5

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-6]]

The conclusion of the explanation of such emptiness is as follows: ^9-6

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-7]]

"Therefore, all phenomena are emptiness," it says. As the Venerable One has stated:
Because there are no phenomena
Apart from the expanse of reality. ^9-7

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-8]]

"Lack of characteristics" means that because the conventional is empty of its own essence, it lacks this or that characteristic; just as a vase does not exist truly, the characteristics of a vase also do not exist truly. As for the ultimate, although it exists by its own essence, the characteristics of the ultimate are not established by way of their own nature, for the three—the characteristic, the defined, and the basis of definition—are merely posited by the mind and do not exist in the nature of reality. Likewise, it is said that because there is no production, there is no cessation. Regarding these two, "unborn" and "unceasing," it is similar to the former: although the conventional has characteristics of production and cessation merely in terms of appearance, they are only delusory appearances. ^9-8

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-9]]

If the conventional itself does not exist from the beginning, then because it lacks causes for production and causes for cessation, there is no production or cessation. As for the ultimate, although it exists as empty of its own essence, because the three—production, cessation, and abiding—are not established within it, it is unborn and ^9-9

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-10]]

Unceasing. Thus, because "emptiness" is the meaning of the emptiness of conditioned phenomena, it is shown that conditioned phenomena, characteristics, production, and cessation are merely imputed and therefore do not exist, while the nature of reality is the meaning of being empty of the imputed. If one thinks that it is not appropriate to treat conditioned phenomena merely as imputed, although it is not the case that whatever is conditioned is necessarily imputed, it is the case that the characteristic of the imputed applies to all conditioned phenomena; for when summarized, all conditioned phenomena are exhausted by the two: object and subject. The appearance of the object being grasped upon the subject, where the subject itself appears to stand independently, is the imputed; thus, this imputed applies to all consciousness. Although the object being grasped appears as an external entity separate from consciousness, it is imputed, and this applies to all objects such as form and all sense faculties such as the eye. It goes without saying that all other existing entities and non-entities that are imputed are also imputed; therefore, the characteristic of the imputed applies to all conditioned phenomena. Thus, the dependent has the characteristic of the imputed, and the imputed also has the characteristic of the dependent. Although the actual consummate is not like that, the consummate as it is imputed is also similar to that. ^9-10

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-11]]

Now, I shall return to the main body of the text. The "stain" is cyclic existence; the stain of cyclic existence, which appears as the arising of various sufferings due to the power of karma and afflictions, does not exist in its own nature. ^9-11

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-12]]

"Separation from the stain" is nirvana. If one attains enlightenment by abandoning obscurations through the cultivation of the path, then because the cultivation of the path is not truly existent and the obscurations are without intrinsic nature, the appearance of being separated from obscurations is merely a way in which the mind arises and does not exist in its own nature. Even though a perfect Buddha is truly established, the appearance of attaining that is a way in which the mind arises, and thus it is merely conventional illusion. Thus, these paths of defilement and purification are dependent natures, and since it is shown that they do not exist in their own nature, the nature of reality is not tainted by the dependent, and the dependent is shown to be empty of its own nature. ^9-12

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-13]]

Regarding that ultimate expanse, although it appears that there is a decrease of qualities during the state of cyclic existence, and that obscurations are abandoned and there is a decrease during the state of nirvana, this is merely a way in which the minds of those in cyclic existence and practitioners arise; there is no decrease in the actual state of reality. ^9-13

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-14]]

The appearance of being filled with faults during the time of cyclic existence, and filled with qualities during the time of nirvana, is also similar to the aforementioned and is not truly established. The reason for this is that because obscurations are not established from the beginning, and qualities exist as one’s own nature from the beginning, the appearance of increase and decrease is merely a false appearance. By this, it is shown that the ultimate, the fully established, is empty of the conventional cyclic existence and nirvana; and although that wisdom of the ultimate, the fully established, pervades all of cyclic existence and nirvana, it is not tainted by cyclic existence or nirvana individually, and because it is without them, it is shown to be permanent as it is without change. This is the third statement that clearly demonstrates the *gzhan stong* (other-emptiness) Madhyamaka. Thus, emptiness, the absence of characteristics, non-arising, non-ceasing, the absence of stains, the absence of separation from stains, the absence of decrease, and the absence of increase are known as the eight profound characteristics; therefore, that wisdom of the *prajñāpāramitā* of the nature of emptiness is [everything] from being emptiness up to the absence of increase. ^9-14

Since this establishes the wisdom of emptiness as the basis and demonstrates that there are no conventional phenomena within it, it is the fourth phrase that clearly demonstrates the great *Madhyamaka* of other-emptiness (*gźan stong*). According to those known as proponents of self-emptiness (*raṅ stoṅ*), the statement "in emptiness there is no form" is untenable, because the basis, emptiness, is not established. According to them, one must alter the sūtra text and recite it as: "The non-existence of form is emptiness." ^11-6

## 13. Mastery of what is possible and what is impossible ^16-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^16-1]]

Sixth, regarding the training in being expert in what is possible and what is impossible: ^16-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^16-2]]

In the wisdom of emptiness, there is no wisdom included within the path, and through that, there is also no definitive emergence. ^16-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^16-3]]

There is also no attainment of the individual states of cause and effect of things, ^16-3

One should grasp the meaning of all these points from the sūtra as follows: for all sentient beings in samsāra and all those who practice the path, the dharmadhātu appears as the various manifestations of the delusions of the basis—such as form—and the phenomena of the path and the result, yet they do not see the true nature of that reality. Therefore, because all those appearances of the basis and path appear while being non-existent, it is taught that at the time of study and reflection, one should determine this, and then, through meditation, one should release all phenomena—from the form aggregate up to non-attainment—into their own state as the essence of awareness, and place the mind in equipoise upon that wisdom of emptiness of inherent nature. ## 15. The Benefits of the Path of Realizing That ^17-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^17-1]]

Third, the benefits of the path of realizing that. ^17-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^17-2]]

"Therefore, O Śāriputra, because bodhisattvas have no attainment," this phrase clarifies the limit; it is evident that this extends to everything up to and including the phrase "there is no form." The reason "attainment" is mentioned primarily is that it is intended to include all other things, meaning that in the nature of reality, neither the cause that produces nor the result that is to be produced—whether suitable or unsuitable for production—is established. Therefore, just as from form up to non-attainment, nothing is established in the actual state of phenomena, so too, because of the necessity for those of the Great Vehicle to meditate in this way, in the very nature of the meditative equipoise and primordial wisdom of bodhisattvas, the entities of cause and effect, along with their actions and functions, have never been established from the very beginning. ^17-2

## 16. Explanation of the fruition realized by that path ^18-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^18-1]]

The fourth, the manner in which the result attained by that path is actualized, is as follows: ^18-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^18-2]]

"In the three times," and so forth, means that all the Buddhas who have resided in the three times—those who have appeared in the past, those who reside in the present, and those who will come in the future—all rely upon this very profound prajñāpāramitā, which possesses the eight characteristics. ^18-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^18-3]]

By meditating according to the six methods of the wise, they have already attained, or will attain, the unsurpassed, perfectly complete enlightenment. In all of these, it is solely the selflessness of phenomena that is explicitly taught, for it is intended that the selflessness of persons arises implicitly from that. However, the statement that bodhisattvas do not meditate on the selflessness of persons means that they do not engage in that manner of meditating on the selflessness of persons which posits the aggregates as the basis of negation and then meditates on the absence of an "I" or "self" upon them; rather, the selflessness of persons that is free from conceptual proliferation is precisely what the heirs of the Conqueror must meditate upon. The fact that no "I" or "self" is established in the ultimate nature is the selflessness of persons, while the fact that no knowable object of appearance or sound has ever existed in the ultimate nature is the selflessness of phenomena; because the former is included within the latter, by meditating on the selflessness of phenomena, the meditation on the selflessness of persons is implicitly accomplished. ^18-3

## 17. Presentation of the dhāraṇī-mantra together with its benefits ^19-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-1]]

The fifth, the explanation of the dhāraṇī-mantra along with its benefits, is as follows: ^19-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-2]]

"Therefore," that is, because of having fully awakened to enlightenment, the vidyā-mantra arises from the luminous aspect of the wisdom that directly realizes the nature of the prajñāpāramitā; it is the causal concordant of the dharmakāya. ^19-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-3]]

The greatness of that mantra is that, because it accomplishes all desired aims and activities, it is a secret mantra of great knowledge, and ^19-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-4]]

The essence of the mantra transcends all worlds and, by suppressing the lesser vehicles, the unsurpassable primordial wisdom itself appears in the form of a vidyā-mantra; and ^19-4

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-5]]

What need is there to mention that this accomplishes other desired activities? It is a mantra that attains the Buddha, who is equal to the unequaled in terms of one's own benefit, and who is without peer in both cyclic existence and peace. ^19-5

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-6]]

It is also a mantra that, for the sake of others, completely pacifies all the suffering of sentient beings. ^19-6

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-7]]

Because the dharmakāya is unerring, it is not false, and because its essence is unchanging and permanent, it is established as true. Since this mantra is also identical in essence to that dharmakāya, it is unerring and not false; therefore, it should be understood as true. This is the meaning of that statement, and it is the fifth phrase that clearly demonstrates the Great Madhyamaka of Other-Emptiness. The actual prajñāpāramitā mantra resides in the mind-stream of the Buddha, and thus it is the actual dharmakāya and the actual established truth. That which appears to the speech and mind of trainees and ordinary beings is not that actual thing, yet because it is a cause for attaining it, it is a truth that is unerring regarding the attainment of the result. However, it is taught that even such mantras as these, spoken by the Victorious One, when they arise in the minds of ordinary beings who have not entered the path, are included within the perfectly established and are untainted. ^19-7

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-8]]

The mantra of the prajñāpāramitā, which possesses such benefits, having been spoken and explained by the noble Avalokiteśvara, is: ^19-8

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-9]]

*Tadyathā* means "it is like this." *Gate* means having gone and realized; its appearing twice signifies the realization of the relative and the realization of the ultimate. *Pāra* means supreme, that is, the abiding nature; *gate* means realized. Again, *pāra* is supreme. *Saṃ* means perfectly; *gate* means realized. *Bodhi* means enlightenment, that is, the primordial wisdom that realizes the abiding nature of the two truths without error; it means having become of one taste with the *dharmadhātu*, which is enlightenment. *Svāhā* means to become established in the basis, and it serves to complete the mantra. ^19-9

## 18. Instruction on Training in the Perfection of Wisdom by Way of Summarizing its Meaning ^20-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^20-1]]

The sixth [section] is the instruction to train in the prajñāpāramitā in the manner of summarizing the meaning. ^20-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^20-2]]

[Regarding] "Śāriputra, bodhisattva," and so forth: having addressed him as "Śāriputra," [it means that] bodhisattva mahāsattvas should train in the profound prajñāpāramitā, which possesses the eight characteristics, by way of such [methods] as the six ways of being skillful. ^20-2

## 19. Resolving doubts through the method of harmonization ^21-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-1]]

The second is the clearing away of doubts by way of [the method of] harmonious correspondence. ^21-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-2]]

[Regarding] "Then the Blessed One," and so forth: after that which has been explained, it shows the manner in which the Blessed One arose from that meditative absorption called "Profound Illumination." ^21-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-3]]

Having given his approval to the bodhisattva mahāsattva, the noble Avalokiteśvara, saying "Excellent!"—this is the brief indication. As it is said:
From wonder, dispute,
Anger, hesitation, and certainty,
And from joy and faith,
A single word appears twice. ^21-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-4]]

With great delight, [he said] "Excellent, excellent!" ^21-4

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-5]]

Having addressed Avalokiteśvara as "son of noble family," [he said], "What you have explained is unerring and is exactly as it is." ^21-5

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-6]]

That is exactly as it is. ^21-6

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-7]]

For that reason, just as you, Avalokiteśvara, have taught, you, the assembly gathered there, such as Śāriputra, must also practice in that way, through the methods of the six ways of being skilled in the profound prajñāpāramitā and so forth. ^21-7

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-8]]

Regarding this explanation by Avalokiteśvara and the practice of the other members of the assembly in accordance with it, not only does the Blessed One Śākyamuni himself now rejoice, but all the Tathāgatas of the ten directions also rejoice. Thus spoke the Teacher, Śākya-siṃha, from his glorious throat. ^21-8

## 20. The Third Root: Rejoicing and Manifest Praise ^22-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-1]]

The third main outline, rejoicing and praising, is as follows: ^22-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-2]]

Regarding "The Blessed One said thus," and so forth: the assembly had been in doubt, wondering if there might be a meaning of the prajñāpāramitā that was superior to what had previously been taught by Avalokiteśvara. Because of this, the Blessed One spoke from his own lips, declaring that even if all the Buddhas of the ten directions were to teach it, the meaning of the prajñāpāramitā is exactly as it is and not otherwise. ^22-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-3]]

The questioner is Śāriputra, and the respondent is the Bodhisattva Mahāsattva, the noble Avalokiteśvara. "Endowed with all" means "all-encompassing," referring to those in the assembly who are endowed with all—that is, all who are diverse in terms of their realms, diverse in terms of their castes, diverse in terms of their migrations, diverse in terms of their forms, diverse in terms of their enjoyments, diverse in terms of their languages, and so forth. These are the ones called the assembly "endowed with all" who rejoice. To mention the distinction between the leaders and the commoners of that assembly endowed with all: there are the gods who dwell in the sky, the humans who dwell on the earth, the asuras who dwell beneath the earth, and the gandharvas who dwell in the three abodes. These four are the leaders, and when mentioning them, it is said that the immeasurable assembly of common worldly beings rejoices and is delighted. ^22-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-4]]

Regarding what the Blessed One spoke in accordance with the explanation by Avalokiteśvara, they placed their trust in it, highly praised it, upheld it with great intent, and firmly grasped it. ^22-4

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-5]]

If the Tathāgata had not spoken such a corresponding confirmation, the assembly would not have trusted it, nor would they have been joyful and satisfied. Furthermore, in accordance with the appearances of other disciples, Śāriputra might have thought, "Was my question perhaps inappropriate?" and Avalokiteśvara might have become disheartened and doubtful, thinking, "Perhaps I did not explain the answer well." However, because the Teacher spoke a corresponding confirmation of "Well done," Śāriputra was joyful because his own question was good, the noble Avalokiteśvara was supremely delighted, knowing that a good answer to his question had been given, and the other members of the assembly also gained trust and were satisfied, their doubts having been resolved.
If someone were to argue, "Since this profound sūtra belongs to the middle turning of the wheel, it is not appropriate for it to teach the Madhyamaka of other-emptiness," or "If it teaches that, then there would be no distinction in profundity between the middle and final turnings," one should respond as follows: The intent of all three turnings of the wheel is indeed the Madhyamaka of other-emptiness, and there are various passages in both the first and middle turnings that clearly teach this through explicit statements. As for the wheel of definitive meaning, it is not that it is hidden, but rather that it is more clear and extensive than both the first and middle wheels, having been brought into the open or spoken of in an immeasurable way. Furthermore, while a distinction of superior and inferior arises regarding the three wheels, they also share a single intent, so those two faults do not exist. Therefore, the direct teaching of this sūtra clearly explains the common other-emptiness, while the uncommon meaning—the Tathāgatagarbha, which is permanent, stable, eternal, and all-encompassing—is explained in the manner of a hidden meaning. ^22-5
