# Vimalamitra’s Extensive Commentary on the Prajñāpāramitāhṛdaya ^0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^0-1]]

The Extensive Commentary on the Noble Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom. ^0-1

## The Introduction ^1-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^1-1]]

In the language of India: Ārya-prajñāpāramitā-hṛdaya-ṭīkā. ^1-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^1-2]]

In the language of Tibet: The Extensive Commentary on the Noble Heart of the Perfection of Wisdom. ^1-2

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^1-3]]

I prostrate to the Bodhisattva Mahāsattva, the Noble Avalokiteśvara, who possesses great compassion. Having prostrated to all the victors of the three times, together with the bodhisattvas, the sole friend of beings, I shall explain the essence of the Mother of the Victors, the supreme and principal meaning of the *Śatasāhasrikā* and the rest. The introduction, the occasion, the inclusion of the teaching, the assembly, the setting, the questions asked, and the answers given—these are summarized into eleven aspects, and together with the agreement and the rejoicing, they are condensed into eight aspects. Regarding that, through the urging of the retinue and the one who perfectly compiles it, ^1-3

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^1-4]]

Having summarized the subject matter to be taught, the introduction is made by the first three words, "Thus have I heard," and so forth. "That which I have heard" means it is "thus" and not otherwise. By this, one pledges that the hearing is authentic, in order to abandon [the faults of] diminution and exaggeration. It is stated that this indicates the authentic taking up and holding [of the teaching] after having heard it once. Therefore, the one who perfectly compiles it is one who has matured the roots of virtue cultivated toward the Buddhas, who is accompanied by the protection of a spiritual friend, and who is to be understood as one who has rendered service to the Buddhas, has asked questions authentically, and has practiced generosity, discipline, and so forth. Otherwise, it would be impossible to hear the essence of the perfection of wisdom of this kind. As the Protector Maitreya has said:
Those who have done much for the Buddhas,
And have cultivated roots of virtue toward them,
Accompanied by the protection of a spiritual friend,
Become vessels for hearing this.
Having served the Buddhas and asked many questions,
Having practiced generosity, discipline, and so forth,
The wise know them to be vessels
For taking up and holding [the teaching].
These [qualifications] are also for the sake of generating faith, undistractedness, and respect in those to be tamed. The third analysis, which appears as "by me," indicates the hearing itself, just as the letters sound; therefore, it completely abandons the self-nature of the cognition that arises from hearing. If it were not so, the phrase "heard by me" would become a sixfold analysis, because it would be the object of cognition. ^1-4

## The Act of Composition ^2-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^2-1]]

Now, in order to reveal the nature of what is heard, the text begins the occasion with the phrase "at one time," and so forth. "Time" refers to the time of the teaching and the like. The phrase "at one time" serves to specify this "time." This is to be connected with the phrase "was dwelling." He is the Bhagavan because he has conquered the four maras; although his qualities and enlightened activities are infinite and he has become the supreme teacher of beings, and although his infinite names have already been praised through his qualities, nevertheless, in order to show that he is the one who is simultaneously worthy of being taken as a refuge by the assembly of the three vehicles who have engaged in the benefit of self and others, the reason for the application of the term Bhagavan is shown to be his ability to cut off the causes of fear. Therefore, in all contexts of explanation, the word Bhagavan is spoken. "Rājagṛha" refers to a specific city in the land of Magadha. In one part of that region is a mountain called Gṛdhrakūṭa, which is the permanent dwelling place of the Buddha, and he was dwelling there. It should be understood that because the Bhagavan was dwelling there, Gṛdhrakūṭa mountain became a stūpa and could not be destroyed by fire or other such disasters, as it is stated in the Bodhisattva-piṭaka. Similarly, in the Ārya-candradīpa-sūtra and others, it is stated: "In this very eastern direction of Rājagṛha, one sees a thousand billion Buddhas; in the presence of all the Victors, this one requested this supreme, peaceful samādhi," and so forth, which is consistent with this. How could the place where the Tathāgata, who is liberated from all faults such as conceptualization and investigation, dwells in any of the four modes of conduct, be destroyed by disasters arising from the power of the Tathāgata? He dwells there, characterized primarily by the divine, noble, and Brahmā-like supreme dwellings, the fourth dhyāna, emptiness, and great compassion; the term "dwelling" signifies that he has already taken up residence. The Bhagavan dwelling there means that he had passed the time of gathering the true Dharma. The specification of the phrase "at one time" indicates that the teacher of beings, who is inclined toward great compassion, dwells in other places at other times. Therefore, those who follow the Bhagavan should also abide by the three excellences, and not by any other means. ^2-1

## Demonstrating the Inclusion of the Teachings ^3-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^3-1]]

The phrase "with a great community of monks" and so forth indicates a gathering that belongs to the teaching. In this regard, they are called "monks" (dge slong) because they have vanquished the defilements. They are called a "community" (dge 'dun) because, being a large assembly of them, they are inseparable. The term "great" (chen po) appears because of their majority, or because of their wisdom, miraculous powers, and so forth. Likewise, there is also "a great community of bodhisattva mahāsattvas." This also indicates greatness in three ways: greatness of mind, being the supreme among all sentient beings; greatness of abandonment; and greatness of realization. "Together with" (dang thabs cig) is synonymous with "along with" (lhan cig tu). Because the act of dwelling is something shared, it is the third distinction according to the definition of the meaning of "together."
Regarding the phrase "gathering that belongs to the teaching," one might ask: who are those who belong to it? Those who have previously cultivated roots of virtue that have now ripened, such that they appear to be capable of listening to such a Dharma and so forth, are those who "belong" to it; this is synonymous with saying they have the fortune to listen to the Dharma and so forth.
If one were to argue that the speaker is authoritative by citing the location, time, and assembly as witnesses, and by speaking in the presence of such witnesses, I do not understand what "greatness" they are speaking of. For if one were to go to those places and ask the witnesses, it would be a long way off for those who lack the capacity to determine the meaning of the sūtra. Furthermore, how can one speak of "witnesses" in a sūtra where the names of the assembly are not even mentioned? Because they are inferior, the assembly of monks is mentioned first, and because they are superior, the assembly of bodhisattvas is mentioned later. This is like the phrase, "the merits of all beings in the ten directions, and of those in training, those beyond training, and the solitary buddhas," and so forth. In some cases, they are arranged in order from the inferior upward. In other cases, they are arranged in order from the superior downward, such as "buddhas and bodhisattvas." Others say that because the assembly is permanent, the assembly of monks is mentioned first, and because they are not permanent, the assembly of bodhisattvas is mentioned later. The claim that "the permanent is to be mentioned first, and others are to be mentioned later" is not a logical position. ^3-1

## Introduction ^4-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^4-1]]

In order to explain the introduction through which this teaching is entered, the words "at that time" and so forth are spoken. "At that time" refers to the moment when the roots of virtue of the assembly are shown to be fully ripened; it is the time when the Blessed One enters into meditative absorption, for the Blessed One is a knower of time. "Dharma" refers to the aggregates and so forth, which are held to be the grasping of their own characteristics. Because it is understood, realized, and known in all ways, or through this, it is called a "discourse on the Dharma." "Profound" refers to emptiness and so forth; that which appears, or that by which it appears, in accordance with the sequence of depending upon one's own or another's continuum, is called "profound appearance." As it is said: "Profound emptiness and so forth, / Free from the extremes of superimposition and denigration, / That is profundity." This indicates that being free from the extremes of superimposition and denigration is to transcend existence and non-existence. Therefore, the master Nāgārjuna has stated: "That which transcends existence and non-existence, / And does not abide in either, / Is the profound, non-conceptual / Meditation on the meaning through the condition of that." "Meditative absorption" (samādhi) is the characteristic of one-pointedness of mind. Entering into its nature for the sake of establishing it correctly is "entering into meditative absorption." In this way, all the activities of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas are primarily for the benefit of others. Therefore, the Blessed One’s entering into this meditative absorption is the arising of the ripening of the mental continuums of all the members of the assembly, each according to their capacity, in accordance with the meaning of the meditative absorption being taught; this should be understood in that way. ^4-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^4-2]]

The Blessed One’s knowledge of the absence of defilements is also established here as a source; for the absence of defilements among the defilements is that when one sees the self, one completely abandons the defilements of people. The absence of defilements of the Victors is that in villages and so forth, they cut off the continuity of those defilements. In order to teach this very meaning below, it is said, "At that time," and so forth. "At that time" means the time when the Blessed One entered into meditative absorption. The word "also" (yang) has the meaning of definitive inclusion; therefore, it has the meaning of "that very reality." Alternatively, the word "also" is for the purpose of inclusion, as it indicates the activity of entering into absorption and includes the activity of insight that appears below. In this explanation, one must include only the time when the roots of virtue of the assembly are fully ripened; if it is so, then the inclusion is logical, but otherwise it is not. Here, a "bodhisattva" is one who is for the sake of awakening, or because they are the awakening-mind itself, they are a "bodhisattva." Since they have entered into buddhahood itself and have attained empowerment, it is appropriate to provide the final etymology for those who have attained it. What, then, is this "awakening"? Awakening is of the nature of space, because all conceptualization has been abandoned. As it is said in the *Vairocanābhisambodhi*: "Awakening is of the nature of space, because all conceptualization has been abandoned." Whoever wishes to realize that is a "bodhisattva," and that is why they are called by that name. Because they have been shown the three qualities—being the supreme of all sentient beings, having a great mind, and so forth—and because of the greatness of the perfection of those who have entered the great grounds, they are "great beings" (mahāsattva). As it is said in the *Ratnaguṇasaṃcayagāthā*: "Great in generosity, great in intellect, and great in power, / Having entered the supreme Great Vehicle of the Victors, / Having donned the great armor and subduing the illusions of Māra, / For that reason, they are well-expressed as 'great beings'." Because they have gone far away from sins and non-virtuous phenomena, they are "noble" (ārya). When one looks with the eye of compassion, sentient beings are "lords" (īśvara); when they are not dependent on the causes of fear and suffering and have become masters, they are called by that name. This is both noble and the Lord who looks with compassion, thus: Ārya Avalokiteśvara. Because they know various aspects or are the supreme of knowledge, they are "wisdom" (prajñā). The nature of the supreme is "supremacy" (pāramitā), and it is supreme indeed. That from which there is no other supreme in the world is explained as supreme; the supremacy of wisdom is the perfection of wisdom. Therefore, it is said: knowledge in the aspect of the emptiness of one's own nature and so forth becomes supreme; whatever possesses the ultimate supreme is the perfection of wisdom. As it is said in the *Noble Eight Thousand Verse Perfection of Wisdom*: "Ananda, if one dedicates the roots of virtue to unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening with wisdom, is that supreme?" Ananda replied, "Blessed One, it is supreme. Sugata, it is supreme." The Blessed One said, "Therefore, because it is supreme, it attains the name 'perfection' (pāramitā)." Other than that, an etymology is not logical, and it is difficult to apply the words, and it would also contradict the Blessed One’s own etymology. ^4-2

## The Eleven Aspects ^5-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^5-1]]

The ultimate *prajñāpāramitā* is the *dharmakāya*, which is characterized by being completely pure in every respect. Other than that, the *prajñāpāramitā* that is the nature of the cause is characterized by the practices of generating the mind, armor, engagement, accumulation, and definitive emergence. The meaning of "profound" has already been explained previously. In this regard, when one speaks of the causal *prajñāpāramitā*, it is the seventh [case] of the object; when one speaks of the resultant [prajñāpāramitā], it is the seventh [case] of the reason. Regarding this, there are many types of practice. On the stage of ordinary beings, the practice on the path of application is the practice of aspiration. On the six stages beginning with the second stage, it is the practice of accomplishment. On the eighth stage, it is the practice of certainty. The practice of aspiration is of three types: for one’s own benefit, for the benefit of both oneself and others, and for the benefit of others; each of these is further divided into nine types, such as the lesser of the lesser, and so on. Thus, there are twenty-seven types of aspiration. The practice of accomplishment is the essence of the three pure trainings, such as the higher morality. Among these, the second stage is the purity of the training in higher morality. The third stage is the purity of the training in higher mind. The training in higher wisdom is of eight types, and through it, the remaining eight stages are encompassed. Among these, the four stages beginning with the Radiant are the purity of the training in higher wisdom, which is fully endowed with the factors of enlightenment, the four noble truths, dependent origination, and the state of abiding without characteristics while exerting effort. Thus, there are six types of practice of accomplishment. The practice of certainty is the eighth stage, which is the purity of the training in higher wisdom that is spontaneously present and without characteristics; it is called the "practice of certainty" because it is certain to engage in the purification of buddha-fields as one wishes. Here, because no specific distinction is made, all practices are included. "Practice" (*spyad pa*) means conduct (*spyod pa*), which is subsequent accomplishment. Alternatively, aspiration itself is the practice, because this is the stage of initiation. It is because this very [aspiration] initiates the eleven stages, such as the Joyous stage. Attainment, perfection, and purification are established by the remaining stages other than that. One observes the object or the conduct for the sake of that; the meaning is that one has entered into accordance with the deeds of the Bhagavan, and there is no other action to be performed. Although other members of the assembly also entered into accordance with the deeds of the Tathāgata, it is shown that the noble Avalokiteśvara entered in that way because he is the speaker. Śāriputra is also [mentioned] because he is the questioner. The phrase "observes" refers to observing in various ways, such as the emptiness of the own-nature; it is analytical investigation. In some texts, the phrase "in this way" appears; at that time, by this [phrase] "in this way," it is intended that the observation is distinguished by the aspects that follow, such as the emptiness of the own-nature, the lack of characteristics, and the non-arising. For that very reason, the syllable "rnam par" (vi-) is added to express the meaning of "various." ^5-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^5-2]]

In order to indicate the meaning of all that is to be contemplated, the phrase "the five aggregates" is spoken. The term "aggregates" also implies the sense sources and the elements, because they are also mentioned when summarizing at the end. Alternatively, the particle "also" (kyaṅ) indicates that one should also contemplate the sense sources and the elements. The statement "these are also empty of intrinsic nature" indicates that they exist just as they are; it is the meaning of being empty of intrinsic nature, being detached, and being selfless. This also signifies the absence of characteristics, the absence of production, and so forth. ^5-2

## Questions ^6-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^6-1]]

The phrase "then" refers here to the immediate sequence. "Through the power of the Buddha" means by the Buddha's power; it is an expression meaning through the blessings and power exerted by the Blessed One. This signifies that the Blessed One is establishing the confidence for the noble Avalokiteśvara to be asked about this meaning. If it were not so, it would indicate that this is not within the scope of Śāriputra. "Venerable" (tshe dang ldan pa) is used because he is worthy of praise for having abandoned the defilements. "Śāri" refers to the woman born into the Śāradvatī lineage, and because he is her son, he is Śāriputra. "As follows" refers to what is stated below. "Spoke" refers to what is about to be said. ^6-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^6-2]]

The phrase "son of noble family" appears for the sake of praise, and because he has attained empowerment, he is a special son of the Buddhas; thus, this is an invocation. The phrase "whoever" indicates that no specific individual is designated. The phrase "wishes to practice" means to engage in practice and to accomplish it perfectly through diligence. "Wishes" is the aspiration for this, and thus it is said in that way. In some texts, it appears as "wishes to perform the practice," which means there is no difference in meaning between "performing" and "accomplishing." By what manner of thought, application, realization, accomplishment, and understanding should one train? One should familiarize oneself with it. ^6-2

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^6-3]]

The phrase "as follows" refers to the previous mode. The phrase "spoke" means "stated." ^6-3

## Response ^7-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-1]]

The phrase "son of noble family or daughter of noble family" appears because this is something that should be stated by the noble ones. The particle "or" indicates mutual dependence, meaning it should be understood as both. The phrase "by that" refers to one who wishes to engage in the practice. The phrase "in this way" refers to the manner that follows below. The phrase "should view" means one should examine each thing in various ways. By this, it expresses the object of observation of special insight, which is an image accompanied by conceptualization. One should also view the object of observation of calm abiding, which is an image that is one-pointed and non-conceptual, in the uninterrupted continuity of that mind. ^7-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-2]]

The phrase "correctly and thoroughly views" means "correctly" because it is unerring, or "equally" because it is non-differentiated due to the absence of characteristics, or "correctly and thoroughly views" because it is a comprehensive and concordant viewing of all phenomena; this is the intended meaning of realizing the object of the limit of reality through calm abiding and special insight. By this, the first stage, which is comprised by the path of seeing the object of the limit of reality, is indicated. Then, beyond that, there are the two types previously taught—the limit of reality and the former—and for the second stage and so on, one should view them as being those three objects of observation. For the tenth stage, which is the final stage, and the stage of the Tathāgata, the object of observation is that very accomplishment of the purpose, because the dharmakāya has been attained. As stated in the noble *Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra*, because one has previously attained calm abiding and special insight, one has attained the objects of observation of the conceptual image and the non-conceptual image. At present, because the path of seeing has been attained, the object of observation of the limit of reality is attained. It is taught that if one keeps in mind those three objects of observation, which are fully endowed with the path of meditation that progresses from one stage to the next, then because all signs and latent tendencies are destroyed, one gradually purifies the mind like gold from one stage to the next, and one also attains the object of observation of the accomplishment of the purpose until the attainment of the unsurpassed, perfectly complete buddhahood; this should be understood in detail from that very text. Here, only a brief application of the example has been stated. ^7-2

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-3]]

How is it that viewing the aggregates and so forth by way of aspects such as the emptiness of their own entities is "correct" because it is unerring, "equal" because they are of a single characteristic by virtue of being without characteristics, and "a correct subsequent realization of all phenomena"? It is because the teaching states, "Form is empty," and since this is the context, it should be connected with the phrase "by way of their own essence." In this way, one understands the two aspects of what appears as form and so forth: that which is an entity and that which is a designation. Among these, those who assert that what appears as form and so forth is an entity are the Vaibhāṣikas, the Sāmkhyas, and others. Those who assert that what appears as an object is a designation are the Sautrāntikas; they say that objects such as blue exist externally, and that experience is the aspect that is cultivated within this consciousness. All those evil webs of conceptualization—which imagine that blue and so forth have their own essence, and which differentiate between atoms, the composite parts initiated by them, consciousness, the primordial substance (pradhāna), and the Brahman of sound—are destroyed by the very negation of their own essence; therefore, that [emptiness] must be stated. That very form which appears clearly and directly to everyone, even down to cowherds and women, is empty of its own essence, just like a city of gandharvas; there is not even the slightest own essence in this appearing form. ^7-3

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-4]]

Therefore, this emptiness of its own essence is what is called "form." By this, the form asserted by the Vaibhāṣikas, the Sautrāntikas, and others is shown to be empty of its own essence. For the Sautrāntikas, because appearances are of the nature of consciousness, the appearance of blue and so forth is empty of the own essence of an external object. ^7-4

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-5]]

If one thinks that because external objects truly exist, emptiness is something other than form, those [objects] are also not other than emptiness, and emptiness is not other than form. ^7-5

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-6]]

The statement "emptiness is also not other than form" serves to refute [the notion of their separation]. Furthermore, if one asks how it is known that the own essence of the aggregates and so forth is non-existent, it is stated here: because the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields are dependently originated, the positions of [their] causelessness and permanence are refuted. Regarding the position of arising from causes, there are two [types of] permanence: either the entity arises simultaneously with its cause, or it arises at a different time. According to the first position, since the cause and effect would be observed at the same time, all distinctions of prior and posterior would be lost, the cause and effect would be inseparable, and an eon would become merely a single year. Regarding the second position, since the cause and effect do not exist together at the same time, there is no efficacy, and thus it would follow that they are causeless. If there is no cause, it would follow that they are either permanently existent or non-existent, because there is nothing else upon which they depend. If they were dependent, the entity would be intermittent. The third position is impossible, because simultaneity and non-simultaneity are mutually exclusive. Therefore, whatever appearances of form and so forth there are, they are empty of their own essence, like the water of a mirage and so forth. Whatever conceptualization there is regarding them—that they arise in dependence upon this or that—is conventional reality. For this very reason, the Blessed One stated in the *Ārya-lalitavistara*: "O son of the Śākya, having seen that the dependently originated phenomena are devoid of own essence, and being fully endowed with a mind like the sky, one is not moved by seeing the deceitful one with his army." And in the *Ratnakūṭa* it is said: "Born from conditions, the own-nature is not born; the body of Dharma is the body of the Victors. The Dharma-nature, permanently abiding like the sky, when shown, results in the attainment of the purification of phenomena." In the *Sarvabuddhaviṣayāvatārajñānālokālaṃkāra* it is also said: "One does not move from the nature of phenomena. Not moving from the nature of phenomena is the attainment of the nature of phenomena. Attaining the nature of phenomena is not to engage in any conceptual proliferation whatsoever. Why is that? Because they are produced by causes and conditions. Whatever is produced by causes and conditions is absolutely unborn. Whatever is absolutely unborn is certainly attained. Whatever is certainly attained does not abide together with the mental engagement of all phenomena," and so forth. For this very reason, the noble Nāgārjuna also said: "Whatever is dependently originated is not born by nature. If it is not born by nature, how can one say it arises? Even regarding the most subtle entities, those who conceptualize them as arising do not see the meaning of what has arisen from conditions, because they are unwise."
Furthermore, the characteristic of the other-dependent nature of conceptualized form is that it is empty of the two characteristics of the conceptualized nature—the grasper and the grasped—at the time of permanence and at the time of constancy; this is the form of the Dharma-nature, the characteristic of the perfected nature. The statement "emptiness is also form" shows that the other-dependent and the perfected are one and the same. Because the own essence of the perfected emptiness and the characteristic of the other-dependent form are certainly grasped as being one, the phrase "emptiness is form" is stated. Thus, having expressed it through the approach of establishing oneness, it also refutes their distinctness through the approach of negation, with the statement "emptiness is not other than form" and so forth. That which is unchangeable and unerringly perfected is the perfected nature; because it is not created, having become efficacious through the gathering of causes and conditions, it is unconditioned. The two [natures] other than that are not established. Because it is not interrupted, it is permanent; this is the Dharma-body of the Great Sage, which exists in the ultimate sense. That alone is the one ultimate truth; this is what is called Nirvāṇa, which has the nature of being non-deluded. It is said that if this were not so, all efforts would be meaningless. Regarding this, the explanation is: what is the nature of the Dharma-body that is desired, such that one says it exists in the ultimate sense? Is it permanent like the self and so forth that are conceptualized by the non-Buddhists, or is it momentary? If it is the first position, how does this differ from the established tenets of the non-Buddhists who assert the end of the Vedas and so forth? How should one understand the statement from the *Candrapradīpa-samādhi*: "All phenomena are always empty of own essence; the sons of the Victors destroy entities. All existence is empty from the beginning; the emptiness of the limited is that of the non-Buddhists"? How should one attribute faults to the non-Buddhists? If it is the second position, then the previous moment is the cause of the subsequent moment, and the faults of simultaneity and non-simultaneity have already been explained, so they will not be repeated.
The statement that "the one alone is the ultimate truth" is a provisional teaching intended to lead those who aspire to Nirvāṇa. As it is said in the *Akṣayamati-nirdeśa*: "If there were no conditioned, what would be negated to make Nirvāṇa true?"—this refutes all such [notions]. In the *Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā-prajñāpāramitā* and so forth, it is also said: "The perfectly complete Buddha is also like an illusion, like a dream. Nirvāṇa is also like an illusion, like a dream. If there were any phenomenon superior to Nirvāṇa, that too would be like an illusion, like a dream." That efforts are meaningful will be shown below. If one does not accept that the non-dual wisdom, which is free from the grasper and the grasped, exists in the ultimate sense, how does it not become non-existent? It does not, because one accepts dependent origination in conventional reality, and in the ultimate sense, it exists as being beyond existence and non-existence. To turn away from existence and grasp at non-existence is the extreme of nihilism, such as saying: "There is no father, no mother, no this world, no world beyond, no ripening of the fruits of well-performed and ill-performed actions," and slandering the truth and the Three Jewels. If one is such a sinner, how could there be any room for the view of the perfectly pure Madhyamaka system? The *Ratnāvalī* also says: "Just as one who goes to a mirage with the thought of water, if one then grasps that 'that water does not exist,' that is ignorance." And the response is also given by the statement: "For one who desires that there is no existence, there is no mind because there is no basis for enlightenment, no acceptance, and no practice; how can one know that it does not exist?" If one says, "Because existence is negated, it becomes non-existence," then why, because non-existence is negated, does it not become existence? If one accepts that non-dual wisdom exists in the ultimate sense, yet one grasps that wisdom as being involved in distinct causes, how is it non-dual? If one does not grasp it as existing in the ultimate sense, how is it omniscience? If you say, "This fault applies to you as well," it does not, because not seeing what exists in the ultimate sense is seeing the reality, and one who does not see water in a mirage is not lacking in knowledge. It is said that "not seeing form is seeing form," and the *Samādhirāja-sūtra* also says: "Not seeing anything is seeing all phenomena." ^7-6

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-7]]

In the same way, just as with form, the aggregates—beginning with feeling and ending with consciousness—are taught, in short, to be empty by their own nature. Therefore, one should understand it in this way: feeling is emptiness; emptiness is feeling. Emptiness is not other than feeling; feeling is not other than emptiness. Likewise, this should be applied to perception and the rest. ^7-7

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-8]]

Having taught that the aggregates are empty by their own nature, in order to also teach this regarding the sense sources and the elements, the words "Śāriputra, in this way" and so forth were spoken. The expression "in this way" refers to the same manner as the aggregates. One should connect this with the passage that states, "All phenomena are the sense sources and the elements, and they are empty by their own nature." ^7-8

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-9]]

Having taught the mode of looking at emptiness by way of own-nature, in order to teach the mode of looking at the lack of defining characteristics, the words "lack of defining characteristics" were spoken. A "defining characteristic" is so called because it indicates or signifies this; for instance, the general defining characteristic of the aggregate of form is "being suitable as form," thus it is form. The general defining characteristic of the aggregate of feeling is "experiencing," thus it is feeling. If one asks what difference there is regarding own-nature, there is a difference made by self-characteristics and general characteristics. A self-characteristic is an own-nature, such as the unique nature of blue and so forth. Because all phenomena of the aggregates, elements, and sense sources lack those general defining characteristics, they are "lacking in defining characteristics." Those things, such as the lack of defining characteristics, are also established without needing to be labored over by their own essence, because they are negated by their own nature. ^7-9

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-10]]

If one asks, "If all phenomena are empty and lack defining characteristics, then how do they arise according to their respective conditions and cease according to their respective conditions?"—it is to show that these [processes], such as "with ignorance as a condition, volitional formations [arise]; with volitional formations as a condition, consciousness [arises]," and so forth, which are the limbs of existence, and the cessation of these limbs of existence, such as "with the cessation of ignorance, volitional formations cease; with the cessation of volitional formations, consciousness ceases," do not exist in this prajñāpāramitā, that the words "no arising, no ceasing" were spoken. ^7-10

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-11]]

If that is the case, then how can the four noble truths—which include the defiled and the purified, along with their causes and results—be valid? It is said, "no stain, no removal of stain," because all phenomena are stainless due to the absence of an own-nature of the stains of the afflictions. ^7-11

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-12]]

It is said to be "naturally free from stains" because there is no purification through the method of removing stains, for all phenomena are luminous by nature. ^7-12

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-13]]

If one asks, "If that is the case, then how is it that on the 'Immovable' stage, because the faults of signs have been abandoned, one abides in the signless spontaneously, thereby diminishing faults, and how is it that the afflictions of the lower realms and total confusion, etc., which were previously abandoned as appropriate, are [not] abandoned?"—it is to show this that the words "no diminution" were spoken; here, "faults" should be understood [as the object of this negation]. ^7-13

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-14]]

Similarly, because the ninth stage and the following three stages respectively attain the individual correct knowledges, the great superknowledges, and the wisdom free from obscurations, the words "no fullness" were spoken to show that there is no increase by way of these qualities. Here, the phrase "by way of qualities" should be understood as being extracted [from the context], for it is stated as such in the *Ratnakūṭa Sūtra* as well. If one asks why only these eight aspects were mentioned and why they are in this order, it is because this explanation is the heart of the *prajñāpāramitā*, and the heart is the supreme and principal part among all other limbs. The principal meaning of the *prajñāpāramitā* is the three doors of liberation, such as emptiness and so forth. These are included within those eight aspects, and those very aspects are the order here: thus, emptiness and signlessness are the two that constitute the concentration of emptiness. The middle four are the concentration of signlessness, as they negate the four—birth, cessation, defilement, and purification. The final two constitute the concentration of wishlessness; here, because faults are abandoned or because one wishes to attain qualities, one makes a wish, and since both of those are negated, it is "wishlessness." ^7-14

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-15]]

Now, in order to show the fruit of having viewed through the mode of instruction, the words "therefore, at that time" and so forth were spoken. "Therefore" means because of having viewed through the mode of instruction. "At that time" means at that moment. One should connect this with the statement "in the correct realization of emptiness, there is no correct viewing of form," for not seeing all phenomena is the realization of emptiness. The statement "no form" and so forth indicates the negation of the aggregates, the sense-fields, and the elements. Among these, the passage from "no form" up to the final "no consciousness" negates the aggregates. The passage beginning with "no eye" up to "no phenomena" negates the sense-fields. ^7-15

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-16]]

From the phrase "no eye element" up to the intermediate words, the fifteen elements—such as the form element and the eye-consciousness element—are implicitly indicated. ^7-16

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-17]]

Because they are the final ones, there are the mind element and the mind-consciousness element. In this way, one should apply the statement "no form" and so forth to everything, including the absence of characteristics, the unborn, the unceasing, and so forth. ^7-17

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-18]]

If, in emptiness, signlessness, and so forth, the aggregates, elements, and sense-fields do not exist, then how is it established that "conditioned by ignorance, compositional factors arise," and likewise that the twelve links arise according to their own conditions and cease through the cessation of those conditions? It is true that they exist conventionally, but because they do not exist in emptiness, signlessness, and so forth, it is said, "no ignorance, no cessation of ignorance." The intermediate words implicitly indicate compositional factors and so forth. "No aging and death." Because that does not exist, there is no cessation of it. ^7-18

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-19]]

If, in the ultimate sense, the links of existence and their cessation do not exist, then how is it that the noble ones see suffering and the rest as truths? That which the noble ones see as truth cannot be incorrect. If those are indeed truths, then how can it be that the links of existence and their cessation do not exist? Because of this, in relation to the emptiness of intrinsic nature and so forth, it is said, "no suffering" and so forth. When the intrinsic nature of suffering does not exist because it is unborn, how could its cause, origination, exist? Therefore, there is also no cessation, and there is no path that serves as the means to that cessation. ^7-19

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-20]]

Because the faults of the nature of signs have been abandoned, it is taught that even wisdom does not exist as established in the ultimate sense; thus, it is said, "no wisdom." In some texts, it also appears as "no ignorance," and its meaning is this: if, because emptiness is free from the faults of the defilements of signs, there is no wisdom, then one might think, "In that case, does the characteristic of ignorance—delusion—exist?" To this, it is said, "no ignorance," for how could ignorance exist in the emptiness of intrinsic nature? ^7-20

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-21]]

Likewise, because the specific perfect understandings, the powers, the fearlessnesses, and so forth, and the intrinsic nature that brings about their attainment do not exist, it is taught that the term "attainment" is not tenable; thus, it is said, "no attainment." ^7-21

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-22]]

Having thus refuted the conceptualization of attainment, it is said, "no non-attainment" in order to refute the conceptualization of its opposite, non-attainment. One should understand that this teaches the profound meaning of being beyond wisdom and ignorance, and beyond attainment and non-attainment, free from the extremes of superimposition and denial. ^7-22

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-23]]

In this way, according to the manner taught above, because there is no dharma whatsoever to be attained in the prajñāpāramitā, ^7-23

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-24]]

the aspiration for enlightenment, the intense longing, and the intention—whatever they may be—are based upon and deeply reliant on the path of meditating on the characteristics of the prajñāpāramitā as it has been taught, and by engaging with exceptional perseverance, ^7-24

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-25]]

It remains so. Regarding enlightenment, there is no dharma whatsoever that is fully established. As it is said in the *Candrapradīpa-samādhi* and other texts, the Blessed One taught the nature of enlightenment: "There is no dharma that is extremely subtle; nor is there anything that can be called 'extremely subtle'." And in the *Vairocana-abhisambodhi*, it is said: "Lord of Secrets, there is no dharma that is extremely subtle, nor is there anything to be apprehended; therefore, it is called unsurpassed, perfectly complete enlightenment." If, in the *prajñāpāramitā* and in unsurpassed, perfectly complete enlightenment, there is no dharma whatsoever, then once the dharmas characterized by faults are eliminated, there is nothing that becomes wisdom. If there is no attainment of what is desired, which is characterized by qualities, then why does one think, having started from enlightenment and relying upon the *prajñāpāramitā*, that one remains [in it]? To the extent that some people are led to engage in something by having first generated hope, they all engage in it for the sake of abandoning the faults they do not desire and for the sake of attaining the qualities they do desire. ^7-25

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-26]]

Since all phenomena are empty of their own nature, if they are not apprehended, who then engages in them? If one asks why one engages and what the fruit of that engagement is, it is said, "because there is no obscuration of the mind," and so forth. "Obscuration of the mind" refers to that which obscures the mind and completely covers it, because it acts as an obstacle to the perfect realization of the true nature of reality as it is. These consist of twenty-two types of extreme delusion, such as attachment to the person and to phenomena, and the eleven discordant classes of their dysfunctional tendencies. The antidotes, which are the means of overcoming them for the sake of abandonment, are the eleven stages, beginning with the Joyous Stage. As it is stated in the *Bhūmipraveśa*: "By the completion of the eleven aspects, there are the eleven stages, such as the Joyous; there are two delusions and one dysfunctional tendency, and the discordant classes are distinct." Likewise, the *Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra* also states: "Regarding the first stage, there is the extreme delusion of attachment to the person and to phenomena, the extreme delusion of the afflictions of the lower realms, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the second, there is the extreme delusion of the errors of subtle downfalls, the extreme delusion regarding the diverse paths of karma, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the third, there is the extreme delusion of desire for sense pleasures, the extreme delusion regarding the perfection of the retention of what has been heard, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the fourth, there is the extreme delusion of craving for meditative absorption, the extreme delusion of craving for phenomena, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the fifth, there is the extreme delusion of conceptualizing turning away from and turning toward cyclic existence, the extreme delusion of conceptualizing turning away from and turning toward nirvāṇa, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the sixth, there is the extreme delusion of the manifestation of conditioned phenomena, the extreme delusion of the arising of many signs, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the seventh, there is the extreme delusion of the arising of subtle signs, the extreme delusion regarding the method of conceptualizing the state of being completely signless, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the eighth, there is the extreme delusion of exertion regarding the signless, the extreme delusion of mastery over signs, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the ninth, there is the extreme delusion of the immeasurable teaching of phenomena, the extreme delusion of the immeasurable words and letters of phenomena, the extreme delusion of the power of retention over higher and higher wisdom and eloquence, the extreme delusion of mastery over eloquence, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the tenth, there is the extreme delusion of the great superknowledges, the extreme delusion of engagement with the secret and the subtle, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies. Regarding the Buddha stage, there is the extreme delusion of attachment to all objects of knowledge, the extreme delusion of hindrance, and the discordant class of their dysfunctional tendencies." Because these are absent, there is no fear for one who abides in the *prajñāpāramitā*; one is not terrified. ^7-26

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-27]]

It is taught that to abide in anything other than prajñāpāramitā is to be afraid of error. This is taught as follows: it is said that a mind that is in error is one that engages. As it is stated in the Pramāṇavārttika: "In order to abandon false superimposition, one exerts oneself even though there is no liberator." Where there is no error, there is no engagement; the Daśabhūmika teaches this, for instance, by stating that when one attains the patience for non-arising phenomena on the eighth bhūmi, it is like the example of a person who dreamt they were being carried away by water and, upon waking, the great effort to be freed from the water ceases, and that very state is their nirvāṇa. Here too, to abide in prajñāpāramitā is to have transcended error just as it is in its own state of confusion; to have gone completely beyond is to have transcended error. ^7-27

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-28]]

Nirvāṇa is the cessation of conceptual proliferation. "Culmination" (mthar phyin pa) comes from the meaning of reaching the end; as it is stated in the Daśabhūmika: "Because the conceptual proliferations abandoned through meditation have been completely abandoned, it is the culmination of purification, for there are no further conceptual proliferations to be abandoned beyond that." Because this nirvāṇa is the culmination of the culmination, or the nature of a mind that has reached the culmination, it is spoken of in that way. Since it is both transcended beyond error and the culmination of nirvāṇa, it is the culmination of nirvāṇa by way of having transcended error; because they rely upon and abide in prajñāpāramitā, the bodhisattvas engage in it. This "greatness of abandonment" indicates the cause for engaging for the sake of that, because it is for the sake of that that they engage. ^7-28

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-29]]

The phrase "in the three times" and so forth is taught for the sake of both the greatness of mind and the greatness of realization, as they have become the supreme among all sentient beings. "Three times" refers to the past, the future, and the present. All the buddhas who reside in those times are buddhas because they possess a special intellect. Since they are both "all" and "buddhas," they are "all the buddhas." Regarding the complete and perfect enlightenment of Vairochana, it is stated: "Having perfectly accomplished the ten bhūmis, one attains the form of mastery. Because of the emptiness and illusion-like nature of phenomena, one is omniscient in this regard, and because one knows the omniscience of all sentient beings, one is called a buddha." The phrase "by the prajñāpāramitā" refers to the nature of truth from which all the wisdom of the tathāgatas arises, and it should be understood as that which is called omniscience. The phrase "relying upon the prajñāpāramitā" refers to that which precedes complete and perfect enlightenment. This omniscience should be viewed as the uninterrupted samādhi; as stated in the Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā-prajñāpāramitā and its instruction, the Abhisamayālaṃkāra: "Attaining buddhahood is uninterrupted; it is the uninterrupted samādhi, which is omniscience. Its object is non-existent, and mindfulness is considered the master. This aspect is peace itself; the absence of characteristics is the characteristic." ^7-29

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-30]]

"Unsurpassed" means that there is nothing higher than this mode of attainment, apprehension, and realization. "Perfectly" means without error. "Complete awakening" means that because there is no defining characteristic, the defining characteristic is one. "Awakening" is realization. "Manifest" is direct perception. "Complete awakening" is to be perfectly awakened. As it is said in the *Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā*: "Blessed One, how is it that there is unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening?" The Blessed One replied, "Subhūti, just as the suchness of all phenomena is, so too is unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening." Subhūti asked, "Blessed One, how is it that the suchness of all phenomena is unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening?" He replied, "Subhūti, just as the suchness from the suchness of form up to the suchness of nirvāṇa is, so too is unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening." Regarding that, the term "unsurpassed" signifies greatness of mind, as it has become the supreme of all sentient beings. The remainder of that signifies greatness of realization. Because one relies upon the *prajñāpāramitā* for the sake of those three aspects of greatness while in the state of a bodhisattva, it is called "for the sake of the three." At the time of perfection, the former indicates the excellence of having abandoned the result, and the latter two indicate the excellence of the realization of the result. Just as a king performs a sacrifice for the sake of certain territories, and the attainment of the territories at the end is the result, likewise, it is by relying upon the *prajñāpāramitā* because one is free from error and because one has attained realization. ^7-30

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-31]]

In order to demonstrate that the prajñāpāramitā itself is the mantra, the words "Śāriputra, therefore" and so forth were spoken.
The defining characteristic of the prajñāpāramitā has already been taught.
A mantra is so called because it is a form of knowledge and because it is a form of protection.
The subsequent words clarify these two meanings. ^7-31

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-32]]

"Great" means that it possesses pervasiveness. Pervasiveness is of two types: pervasiveness in time and pervasiveness in space. In this way, it enters into all phenomena and all times without exception. By this, it is shown that this is distinct from the mantras created by Īśvara and others. As it is said in the *Vairocanābhisambodhi*:
"It is completely beyond the three times,
It is the existence that arises dependently.
It is the result, both seen and unseen,
Existing in mind, speech, and body.
The result is said by the worlds
To be the measure of a single eon.
The perfect Buddha has explained
That the mantra transcends the eon of results.
The meditative absorption of the Buddhas,
The great sages, and the Bodhisattvas,
Is pure, having abandoned all marks,
And is endowed with the marks of the worlds.
Even the ripening matures here;
Results are obtained through actions.
At the time when attainment is reached,
At that time, actions are reversed.
Because the mind has no intrinsic nature,
And because results and causes are abandoned,
Even from the births of actions,
Birth is like the sky," so it is said.
By this, it is called *vidyā* (knowledge) because one knows and understands, or because one attains the fulfillment of one's wishes. Because it is both *vidyā* and "great," it is *mahāvidyā*. Because *mahāvidyā* itself is the mantra, it is the *mahāvidyā-mantra*. ^7-32

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-33]]

Therefore, because there is nothing else more supreme than this, it is the *anuttara-guhyamantra* (unsurpassed secret mantra). ^7-33

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-34]]

That which is "unequaled" is space, because there is nothing equal or similar to it. Because it is equal to that, it is "equal to the unequaled." In the *Bodhisattva-piṭaka*, the Blessed One himself has spoken the words "equal to the unequaled." Furthermore, in the *Tathāgatācintyaguhyanirdeśa*, it is said:
"If one makes any analogy
For the Buddha-dharma,
That is not in accordance with the Buddha;
That is to disparage the Victorious One.
Whatever is equal to the Buddha-dharma,
For which there is a single analogy,
Is taught as the element of space,
Which has no measure whatsoever,"
and so forth. By this "equal to space," one should understand pervasiveness, non-obstruction, non-interruption, and so on. By these, the meaning of *vidyā* (knowledge) is shown. ^7-34

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-35]]

In order to show the meaning of "protection," it is said, "all suffering," and so forth. It is the *guhyamantra* (secret mantra) that completely pacifies all suffering, because it completely pacifies and cuts the continuity of all sufferings of the nature of suffering, such as the suffering of conditioning, the suffering of change, and the suffering of suffering itself. ^7-35

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-36]]

Having thus shown the meaning of the etymology of *guhyamantra*, in order to show the meaning of the etymology of that which is to be expressed secretly, it is said, "it is true, not false," and so forth. "True" is *tathatā* (suchness), because it is exactly as it is in reality. That which is exactly as it is in reality is "not false." Therefore, it should be understood as the *mantra* of the *prajñāpāramitā*. By this, it is taught that just as for a person of dull faculties who is unable to see through the way things are taught, it is taught secretly, so too should they grasp the *prajñāpāramitā* as a *guhyamantra* exactly as it is. ^7-36

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-37]]

The etymology of the term *guhyamantra* (secret mantra), meaning that which is to be explained secretly, depends on what this expression refers to. Alternatively, because it resides in the mind and acts as a protection, it is a *guhyamantra*. One should keep the *prajñāpāramitā* in mind with reverence and without interruption. Because it is logically sound, it is valid; therefore, what is to be known must be firmly grasped. ^7-37

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-38]]

The occurrence of the phrase "in this way" means that one should train according to the manner that has been taught. ^7-38

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-39]]

The occurrence of the phrase "then" refers here to without interruption. "Arising from that meditative concentration" refers to what was taught previously. The occurrence of the phrase "having arisen" means having released it with the mind. ^7-39

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-40]]

Bestowing approval upon the teacher by saying "Excellent," and also expressing agreement with the explanation. ^7-40

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-41]]

The nature of bestowing approval by saying "Excellent" is the occurrence of the phrase "Excellent, excellent, noble son." The repetition of the phrase twice is for the sake of joy or extreme joy. ^7-41

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-42]]

The occurrence of the phrase "It is just so," and so forth, expresses agreement with the explanation. ^7-42

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-43]]

It is just so, and not otherwise. ^7-43

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-44]]

Why is that? It is because the passage "One should train in the prajñāpāramitā," and so forth, was spoken. If one asks what that is like, it is said, "It is just as you have taught," which is to say, the intention is that one should train without contradicting what you have taught. ^7-44

## Rejoicing ^8-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-1]]

In this way, the Blessed One, having shown his own agreement with the teaching given by the noble Avalokiteśvara, stated that "the Tathāgatas also rejoice," in order to show that other Tathāgatas are also truly delighted with whatever you have taught. The phrase "rejoice" is a confirmation of the meaning that must inevitably be practiced; the Tathāgatas are those who inevitably take delight in such an explanation as this. By these two, agreement and rejoicing, it is also shown that this Tathāgata is worthy of being delighted with this explanation. ^8-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-2]]

For that very reason, it is said, "When the Blessed One had spoken these words." The phrase "these words" begins from the inquiry of Śāriputra and extends up to the words, "the Tathāgatas also rejoice." The phrase "had spoken" refers to the Blessed One having spoken. Although the noble Avalokiteśvara is the one who is teaching here, it is nonetheless the word of the Tathāgata himself; for in this way, through the blessing of the Tathāgata, everything that is taught by the śrāvakas and others is also the word of the Tathāgata. If that is so, then there is no need to even mention the teachings of the noble Avalokiteśvara, Samantabhadra, Mañjuśrī, and others, who have truly entered into the state of Buddhahood. The meaning of this has already been explained by me previously. The phrase "rejoice" refers to the joy and delight that arises from hearing and explaining the meaning of the essence of the prajñāpāramitā, which is the supreme and principal meaning among the topics to be expressed in the *Śatasāhasrikā*. ^8-2

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-3]]

This should be applied to Śāriputra, the noble Avalokiteśvara, and others. Some also apply it to the Blessed One, for at that time, the disciples’ holding of the *prajñāpāramitā* as the principal meaning should be viewed as the cause of [the Blessed One’s] delight. The phrase "the entire assembly" is stated for the sake of inclusion. By this, rejoicing and high praise are also to be included. Because the bodhisattvas, bhikṣus, bhikṣuṇīs, upāsakas, and upāsikās are all present here, it is called an assembly that possesses everything. Gods are the Four Great Kings and so forth. Humans are well known. Asuras are so called because they have abandoned the liquor that arose from the churning of the ocean of milk. They are the sons of Dāna and the sons of Dānu. Gandharvas are those who sing the songs of the gods, such as Dhṛtarāṣṭra and others. Because they are present together with the gods and the rest, it is the world including gods, humans, asuras, and gandharvas. Because the gods are the principal ones, they are mentioned first. It is called the "world" (*jagat*) because it is perishable, and it is the nature of the five aggregates. ^8-3

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-4]]

What the Blessed One has spoken is the very nature of the explanation. "Highly praised" means to be endowed with great joy and to hold it with delight. ^8-4

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-5]]

Whatever merit I have attained
From explaining the essence of the *prajñāpāramitā*,
May that cause all the worlds
To become the *prajñāpāramitā* themselves.
In the presence of the *saṅgha* of bhikṣus
Residing in the monastery of the source of Brahmā,
This extensive commentary on the essence
Of the *prajñāpāramitā* was composed by Vimalamitra. ^8-5

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-6]]

The extensive commentary on the noble *prajñāpāramitā-hṛdaya*, composed by the Indian master Vimalamitra, is completed. ^8-6

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-7]]

It was translated, edited, and finalized by the Indian master Vimalamitra himself, along with the Tibetan chief editor-translators, the monks Namkhai Nyingpo, Yeshé Nyingpo, and others. ^8-7
