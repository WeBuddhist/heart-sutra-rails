# The Wondrous Word Commentary on the Prajñāpāramitā Heart Sūtra by Jonang Jetsun Tāranātha ^0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^0-1]]

A Wondrous Word-by-Word Commentary on the Prajñāpāramitā Hṛdaya Sūtra. Word-by-Word Commentary on the Heart Sūtra. ^0-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^0-2]]

Namo Buddhāya. To explain just a fraction of the meaning of the Prajñāpāramitā Hṛdaya Sūtra: Although the term 'prajñāpāramitā' applies to many meanings, the actual and primary one is the non-dual wisdom of the fundamental nature of all knowable phenomena, which is the sugatagarbha. This sūtra teaches the nature of that prajñāpāramitā, presenting these three primary points of meaning: the characteristic of being free from conceptual elaboration, the characteristic of being unchanging, and the characteristic of having all aspects. Because its words are extremely few, it is the 'heart'. If one asks how it teaches these: 'no form' and so forth teaches freedom from elaboration; 'unproduced' and so forth teaches being unchanging; and 'form is emptiness' and so forth teaches having all aspects. ^0-2

## 1. The words of the introduction ^2-0

'Thus' and so forth: At the location, Mount Gṛdhrakūṭa within Rājagṛha, the Teacher, the Bhagavān Śākyamuni, the retinue of monks—that is, a great assembly of the saṅgha of śrāvaka arhats—and a vast saṅgha of noble bodhisattvas gathered from various buddha-fields—'together with' means residing together, and at 'one time,' when the entire retinue had become vessels for this Dharma and their mindstreams were ripened, 'I,' the compiler Vajrapāṇi, 'heard' the words of the Dharma, the Prajñāpāramitā Hṛdaya, 'thus,' without error. ## 2. The door through which the dharma of the sūtra arises ^3-0

'At that time, the Bhagavān' and so forth: Because it is difficult to realize, it is 'profound', which is the fundamental nature, prajñāpāramitā. The pristine wisdom that directly realizes it is 'illumination', which means actually seeing it. The meditative equipoise endowed with that pristine wisdom is 'samādhi'. Although the Bhagavān constantly resides in that samādhi, to the perception of the trainees gathered there, it appeared as if he newly entered into meditative equipoise in that samādhi of profound illumination. ^3-1

## 6. Establishing natural prajñāpāramitā ^9-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-1]]

'Form is emptiness' and so forth: If one applies this to the two truths in common, in this instance where the aggregate of form appears to ordinary infantile beings right now, the aggregate of form is non-existent, because that form is empty of its own essence, except as a mere deluded appearance. Therefore, from the isolated perspective of form, it is an emptiness of being non-existent and 'is not'. ^9-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-2]]

That very dharmatā of emptiness dwelling in the place of form is emptiness proper; this is the emptiness of being true, existent, and 'is'. Even though the dharmatā of emptiness that exists in the fundamental nature appears as the aggregate of form to ordinary infantile beings, it is non-existent by essence, except as a mere deluded appearance. ^9-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-3]]

If form is taken as the substratum, the dharmadhātu of emptiness does not exist separately other than it, because the very instance where the appearance of form arises is the dharmadhātu, and because the substratum, the aggregate of form itself, has never existed, ^9-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-4]]

because it is incapable of establishing its own position as distinct from the dharmatā of the aggregate of form, and because the awareness of dharmatā is also free from conceptual elaboration, thus it does not apprehend the characteristic of being a distinct counterpart in opposition to form. If that very dharmadhātu of emptiness is apprehended as the substratum, form does not exist other than it, because even though dharmatā exists, the aggregate of form is non-existent, and also because of the former reasons. For example, if someone with jaundice looks at a conch shell, they will not see it as a coiled white object, but will see it as a coiled yellow object. Even if seen as such, that coiled yellow is non-existent from the beginning except as a mere deluded appearance, whereas the white exists; thus, considering them to be one in the sense that there is no separate basis between that which appears as yellow and the white, it is like saying: 'That which is seen as yellow is nothing other than the conch shell. The conch shell is that yellow seen by you. Other than that yellow seen by you, there is no conch shell. Other than the conch shell, that yellow seen by you is not established.' This fits the meaning extremely well. According to the proponents of rangtong, since they assert that emptiness is pervaded by the ultimate, it would absurdly follow that the aggregate of form is the ultimate truth; and while it would be reasonable to state 'emptiness is not form, nor is it other than form', it is inappropriate to state 'form is emptiness, emptiness is form'. Thus, this is the second statement that clearly demonstrates the great Madhyamaka of zhentong. Explaining it in this way is to teach it in accordance with what is clearly shown as the words of negation in the explicit teaching of this sūtra. If explained according to the hidden meaning, it is exclusively a word of affirmation: 'Whatever is the ultimate form of dharmatā is the pristine wisdom of emptiness; whatever is the pristine wisdom of emptiness appears as the form of dharmatā'. Having taught this, one might understand that those two merely have a common locus, but in order to refute the doubt that the pristine wisdom of emptiness might not be ultimate form, and that ultimate form might not be the pristine wisdom of emptiness, it says 'other than form' and so forth. In this context, 'emptiness' is not a mere bare emptiness, but the pristine wisdom empty of duality. The ultimate aggregate of form is indeed not the aggregate of form, but that ultimate aspect of the aggregate of form within the dharmadhātu of all aspects is called the 'aggregate of form of dharmatā' or the 'ultimate aggregate of form'. ^9-4

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-5]]

The subsequent ones should also be understood by the same logic, applying it as: 'In the same way, feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are emptiness. Emptiness is feeling up to consciousness. Emptiness is not other than feeling up to consciousness. Feeling up to consciousness are not other than emptiness.' ^9-5

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-6]]

Summarizing the explanation of such emptiness: ^9-6

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-7]]

Therefore, it says 'all phenomena are emptiness', which is as the Venerable One said: 'Because there are no phenomena apart from the dharmadhātu, for that reason.' ^9-7

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-8]]

As for 'without characteristics': because the relative is empty of its own essence, it does not have this or that characteristic; just as a vase does not truly exist, the characteristic of a vase also does not truly exist. As for the ultimate, although it exists by its own essence, the characteristic of the ultimate is not established by nature, because the three—characteristic, characterized, and basis—are mere mental imputations and do not exist in the fundamental nature. Similarly, because there is no birth, it says 'there is no cessation'. These two, 'no birth' and 'no cessation', are also like the former: for the relative, although the characteristics of birth and cessation exist merely in the perspective of appearance, they are mere deluded appearances; ^9-8

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-9]]

if the relative itself has been non-existent from the beginning, there are no causes for it to be born or causes for it to cease, and therefore there is no birth or cessation. As for the ultimate, although emptiness exists by its own essence, because the three—birth, cessation, and abiding—are not established in it, it is unborn and ^9-9

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-10]]

unceased'. Thus, since emptiness has the meaning of being empty of the conditioned, conditioned things, characteristics, birth, and cessation are taught to be non-existent because they are mere imaginary imputations, and dharmatā is the meaning of being empty of the imaginary. If one thinks it is inappropriate to treat mere conditioned things as imaginary: although it is not pervaded that if something is conditioned it must be imaginary, it is pervaded that the characteristic of the imaginary applies to all conditioned things. In short, all conditioned things can be reduced to just two: object and subject. Upon the subject, the appearance that the subject itself is independently established separately from the apprehended object is imaginary, and thus that imaginary characteristic applies to all consciousnesses; while the appearance that there are external objects as apprehended objects separate from consciousness is also imaginary, and that itself applies to all objects like form and all faculties like the eye. Other than those, it goes without saying that all existent things that are imputedly existent and all non-existent things are imaginary. Therefore, the characteristic of the imaginary applies to all conditioned things. Therefore, the dependent has the characteristic of the imaginary, and the imaginary also has the characteristic of the dependent. Although the actual perfect is not like that, the imputed perfect is also similar to it. ^9-10

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-11]]

Now, to enter into the main text: 'defilements' refers to saṃsāra; the defilement of saṃsāra, which appears as the arising of various sufferings through the power of karma and afflictions, is also non-existent by its own essence, and ^9-11

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-12]]

'free from defilements' refers to nirvāṇa. If one attains awakening having abandoned obscurations through meditating on the path, meditating on the path also does not truly exist, and since obscurations have no nature, the appearance of being free from obscurations is also nothing more than a mode of mental arising and is non-existent by essence. Even though the perfect Buddha is truly established, the appearance of attaining it is a mode of mental arising, and therefore it is a mere relative illusion. Thus, these paths of thorough affliction and complete purification are dependent, and since they are taught to be non-existent by essence, it is taught that dharmatā is untainted by the dependent, and that the dependent is empty of its own essence. ^9-12

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-13]]

For that ultimate dhātu, although it appears that qualities decrease during the phase of saṃsāra, and that obscurations are abandoned and decrease during the phase of nirvāṇa, this is a mode of arising in the minds of saṃsāric beings and yogis; but in the fundamental nature, there is no decrease, and ^9-13

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^9-14]]

the appearance of being filled with faults during the time of saṃsāra, and being filled with qualities during the time of nirvāṇa, is also like the former: not truly established. The reason for this is: because obscurations are unestablished from the beginning, and because qualities are inherently present from the beginning, the appearance of increasing and decreasing is a mere mistaken appearance. By this, it is taught that the ultimate perfect is empty of the relative saṃsāra and nirvāṇa; and although that perfect ultimate pristine awareness is all-pervasive throughout saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, it is untainted by saṃsāra and nirvāṇa respectively, and because it is without that, there is no transition or change, and therefore it is taught to be permanent. This is the third statement that clearly teaches other-emptiness Madhyamaka. Thus, 'emptiness', 'without characteristics', 'unborn', 'unceased', 'without defilements', 'without freedom from defilements', 'without decrease', and 'without increase'—these are known as the eight profound characteristics. Therefore, that pristine awareness of the natural emptiness of prajñāpāramitā is everything from being 'emptiness' up to 'without increase'. ^9-14

## 13. Becoming skilled in the possible and the impossible ^16-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^16-1]]

Sixth, training in skill regarding the possible and the impossible: ^16-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^16-2]]

In the pristine awareness of emptiness, there is no pristine awareness subsumed by the path, and there is no definite emergence through it. ^16-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^16-3]]

There is also no attainment of the respective states of causes and effects of things, and ^16-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^16-4]]

there is also no non-attainment of the state of the result from what is not a cause. The meaning of the sūtra regarding all of these should be grasped as follows: For saṃsāric sentient beings and all meditators on the path, the dharmadhātu arises as the illusion of foundational phenomena such as form, and as various appearances of the phenomena of the path and the result, and they do not see it in the actual form of that abiding nature. Therefore, since all those appearances of the foundation and the path appear while being non-existent, having established them as such during the time of hearing and reflection, one should, through meditation, let all phenomena from the aggregate of form up to non-attainment dissolve naturally into the essence of awareness, and this demonstrates that one must rest in meditative equipoise within that pristine awareness of natural emptiness. This shares the same essential point as explaining the three absences of nature, because in the established meaning of the explanation by way of three, the ultimate expanse existing perfectly arises solely as being merely empty of adventitious conventionalities. Therefore, the thought of some who consider those two ways of explanation to be contradictory is a display of great arrogance due to not understanding. Thus, among the six, by training in the three—aggregates, elements, and sense sources—one becomes skilled in the defining characteristics of dharmatā; by training in dependent origination and the four truths, one becomes skilled in adopting, discarding, engaging, and turning back; and by training in the possible and the impossible, one becomes skilled in causes, conditions, and results. ^16-4

## 15. The benefits of the path that realises that ^17-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^17-1]]

Third, the benefits of the path that realizes that: ^17-1

Therefore, just as nothing from form up to non-attainment is established in the abiding nature of things, practitioners of the Mahāyāna must meditate in that way; "therefore," in the very essence of the pristine awareness in meditative equipoise of the bodhisattvas, because things that are causes and results, along with actions and agents, are primordially unestablished, ^17-2

even the two obscurations along with their habitual tendencies, which arise in one's own mind from the perspective of illusion, are primordially non-existent in their basic nature, and because fright and fear caused by the suffering of saṃsāra and by karma and afflictions are also never existent in their basic nature, ^17-3

## 16. Explaining the result realised by that path ^18-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^18-1]]

Fourth, the manner of actualizing the result attained by that path: ^18-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^18-2]]

"The three times" and so forth: manifestly abiding in the three times, that is, all the Buddhas who appeared in the past, who abide in the present, and who will come in the future, relying upon this very profound prajñāpāramitā endowed with eight characteristics, ^18-2

In the explicit teaching of all of those, solely the selflessness of phenomena is stated; the selflessness of persons is intended to arise by implication of that. However, the statement that seems to say the bodhisattva does not meditate on the selflessness of persons means that having asserted the aggregates, the basis of negation, to exist, they do not engage in such a manner of meditating on the selflessness of persons that meditates on the absence of 'I' and 'self' upon them; rather, the selflessness of persons that has the aspect of freedom from conceptual elaboration is indeed an object to be meditated upon by the sons of the victorious ones. ## 17. Teaching the dhāraṇī-mantra along with its benefits ^19-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-1]]

Fifth, teaching the dhāraṇī-mantra along with its benefits: ^19-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-2]]

"Therefore, the prajñā..." and so forth: because they manifestly and perfectly awaken in that way, within the apparent aspect of the pristine awareness that directly realizes the natural prajñāpāramitā, the awareness-mantra arises; it is the natural outflow of the dharmakāya. ^19-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-3]]

The greatness of that mantra is: because it accomplishes all desired aims and activities, it is the secret mantra of great awareness, and ^19-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-4]]

because the essence of the mantra transcends the entire world and outshines the inferior vehicle, it is unsurpassed pristine awareness itself arisen in the aspect of an awareness-mantra, and ^19-4

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-5]]

what need is there to even mention that it performs other desired activities? For one's own benefit, it is "equal to the unequaled," meaning it is a mantra that causes the attainment of Buddhahood, which is free from any rival in both existence and peace. ^19-5

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-6]]

For the benefit of others, it is also a mantra that completely pacifies all the suffering of sentient beings. ^19-6

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-7]]

Because the dharmakāya is undeceptive, it is "not false," and because its own essence is unchanging and permanent, it is truly established; and because this mantra is also of the same essence as that dharmakāya, it is undeceptive and not false, and thus it "should be known as the truth." That is the meaning, and this is the fifth statement clearly demonstrating the great Madhyamaka of other-emptiness. The actual mantra of prajñāpāramitā is that which abides in the mind-stream of a Buddha, so it is the actual dharmakāya and is actually truly established; and that which appears to the speech and mind of trainees and ordinary beings, although not actual in that way, is the cause for attaining that, so it is indeed a truth that does not deceive in the attainment of the result. However, it is taught that mantras such as this spoken by the victorious ones belong to the consummate nature and are undefiled, even down to when they arise in the minds of ordinary beings who have not entered the path. ^19-7

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-8]]

The mantra of prajñāpāramitā endowed with those benefits, spoken and explained by the noble Avalokiteśvara, is: ^19-8

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^19-9]]

"tadyathā" means "it is thus." "gate" is gone and realized. Appearing twice means realizing the relative and realizing the ultimate. "para" is supreme, which is the abiding nature. "gate" is realized. Again, "para" is supreme; "sam" is perfect; "gate" is realized. "bodhi" is awakening, which is the pristine awareness that unmistakably realizes the abiding nature of the two truths. It means awakening, becoming of one taste with the dharmadhātu. "svāhā" means "establish the foundation," which accomplishes the end of the mantra. ^19-9

## 18. Advising to train in prajñāpāramitā by way of summarizing the meaning ^20-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^20-1]]

Sixth, the advice to train in prajñāpāramitā by way of summarizing the meaning is: ^20-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^20-2]]

"Śāriputra, a bodhisattva" and so forth: having called out, "Śāriputra," [he says that] bodhisattva great beings should train in the profound prajñāpāramitā endowed with eight characteristics by way of such things as the six ways of being skilled. ^20-2

## 19. Clearing away doubt by way of expressing approval ^21-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-1]]

Second, clearing away doubt by way of expressing approval is: ^21-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-2]]

"Then the Bhagavan" and so forth: then, after it was explained like that, the Bhagavan demonstrated the manner of arising from that samādhi called "Profound Illumination," and... ^21-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-3]]

Having briefly taught this by saying, "[he] gave the approval 'excellent' to the bodhisattva great being, the noble Avalokiteśvara," it is as was said: "From wonder, dispute, anger, discouragement, certainty, joy, and faith, a single word occurs twice." ^21-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-4]]

Out of supreme joy, [he said,] "Excellent, excellent." ^21-4

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-5]]

Having called out to Avalokiteśvara, "O child of noble family," [he said,] "what you have explained is unmistakably exactly like that. ^21-5

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-6]]

It is exactly like that. ^21-6

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-7]]

For that reason, just as you, Avalokiteśvara, have taught, you, the retinue gathered there, Śāriputra and so forth, should also practice that way of practicing in the profound prajñāpāramitā by way of such things as the six ways of being skilled. ^21-7

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^21-8]]

Not only do I, the Bhagavan Śākyamuni, now rejoice in this explanation by Avalokiteśvara and this corresponding accomplishment by the other retinues, but all the tathāgatas of the ten directions also rejoice." So the teacher Śākyasiṃha spoke from his glorious throat. ^21-8

## 20. The third main section: rejoicing and praising ^22-0

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-1]]

The third of the root [topics], rejoicing and openly praising, is: ^22-1

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-2]]

[The passage beginning,] "The Bhagavan having spoken thus..." and so forth: While the retinues had doubts, thinking, "Is there any meaning of the prajñāpāramitā beyond this previously taught by Avalokiteśvara?", the Bhagavan spoke from his presence, "Even if all the buddhas of the ten directions were to teach it, the meaning of the prajñāpāramitā is exactly like that and not otherwise," and ^22-2

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-3]]

the questioner Śāriputra, and the answerer, the bodhisattva great being, the noble Avalokiteśvara, and "endowed with all" means possessing all; so it means that "those retinues rejoiced, being endowed with all such things as different regions, different families, different migrations, different forms, different resources, different languages, and so forth". If one mentions the specific differences of the principal and ordinary members of that retinue endowed with all: the devas who move in the sky, the humans who move on the surface of the earth, the asuras who move beneath the earth, and the gandharvas who move in the three places—by mentioning those four principal ones, the immeasurable ordinary worldly retinues rejoiced, being joyful and delighted; ^22-3

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-4]]

Placing trust in and openly praising that explanation by Avalokiteśvara and the concordant speech by the Bhagavan, they hold it in high esteem and make it a certainty. ^22-4

![[bo-taranatha-tsikdrel#^22-5]]

Here, if the Tathāgata had not spoken his concordant approval in that way, the retinues would not have gained belief and thus would not have been joyful and satisfied. Furthermore, in accordance with the perceptions of other disciples, Śāriputra would have been discouraged and doubtful, thinking, "Was my question inappropriate?"; and Avalokiteśvara too would have been discouraged and doubtful, thinking, "Is it possible that I did not explain the answer well?" However, since the Teacher spoke the concordant approval, "Excellent," Śāriputra was joyful that his own question was good; the noble Avalokiteśvara too was supremely joyful, knowing that his answer to the question turned out well; and the other retinues also gained delight and satisfaction, since they gained belief and their doubts were severed. Moreover, if some were to say here, "If this profound sūtra belongs to the sūtras of the middle turning, it is inappropriate for it to teach the Madhyamaka of other-emptiness," and, "If it teaches that, there would be no difference in profundity between the middle and final [turnings]," they should be answered as follows: The intention of all three turnings is also the Madhyamaka of other-emptiness, and even in the first and middle turnings there are some words that clearly teach it explicitly. In the turning that ascertains the ultimate, it is taught more clearly and extensively, without being hidden but made manifest or expounded boundlessly, than in both the [first] turning and the middle one. Thus, there is a difference of superior and inferior among the three turnings, and since their intention is also the same, those two faults do not apply. Therefore, regarding the explicit teaching of this sūtra, it clearly teaches the common other-emptiness, while it explains the uncommon meaning of the heart of the Sugata, which is permanent, steadfast, eternal, and possessing all aspects, by way of hidden meaning. ^22-5

## 21. The conclusion, virtuous in the end, and the colophon ^23-0
