# Extensive Commentary on the Noble Prajñāpāramitā Heart Sūtra by Ācārya Praśāstrasena ^0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^0-1]]

In the language of India: Āryaprajñāpāramitāhṛdayaṭīkā. ^0-1

## Explanation of This Sūtra ^1-0

### The Name of Prajñā ^1-1-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-1]]

In the Tibetan language: Extensive Commentary on the Heart of the Noble Prajñāpāramitā. Homage to youthful Mañjuśrī and to noble Avalokiteśvara. In explaining this sūtra, it is elucidated through ten topics: the name of prajñā, the introduction, the absorption, the initiation of the dialogue, engaging in prajñā, the defining characteristic of prajñā, the domain of prajñā, the qualities of prajñā, the result of prajñā, and the mantra of prajñā. As for the name of prajñā: if there were no name, entities could not be known; therefore, the name is stated in order to apprehend the entity. Thus, it is called the "Heart of the Noble Prajñāpāramitā." Regarding that "knowledge": there is worldly, transcendent, and unsurpassable knowledge. Worldly knowledge is corrupted: it perceives the impermanent as permanent, the impure as pure, suffering as happiness, and the selfless as having a self. Transcendent knowledge is the knowledge of śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas that persons are selfless: it is knowing that conditioned things are impermanent, conditioned things are suffering, persons are selfless, and nirvāṇa is peace. Unsurpassable knowledge is the Tathāgatas' knowledge that persons and phenomena are selfless: it is knowing them as signless, wishless, and empty. "Knowledge" here refers to unsurpassable knowledge. "Noble" means being far removed from sorrow and suffering through this knowledge. "Supreme" means being the supreme knowledge among the worldly and the transcendent, and since it is unsurpassable, it is "supreme knowledge" (prajñā). "Gone beyond" (pāramitā): birth, death, and suffering are the near shore; nirvāṇa is the far shore; and sentient beings driven by the desires of saṃsāra are in between. Since this prajñā acts as a boat and a ship to bring them to the shore of nirvāṇa, it is "gone beyond." "Heart": it is called the "heart" because all the sūtras of the Prajñāpāramitā in one hundred thousand stanzas are contained within this. Alternatively, because the ultimate prajñāpāramitā is taught by the Tathāgata as supreme within the teachings, it is called the "heart". ^1-1

### The Setting ^1-2-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-2]]

This is the introduction. If it were not taught, one would not know where and to whom it was taught, so the introduction is stated in order to teach the location where and to whom it was taught. "Thus" refers to the matters appearing below; this eliminates exaggeration and deprecation. "By me" indicates that it was heard directly; it indicates that the Dharma was merely heard directly, but the meaning was not realized. "Heard" means that it was apprehended by the auditory consciousness of the ear faculty, which is capable of hearing. "At one time" means that it was heard on a single occasion, during the time of the morning or the afternoon. "Bhagavān", because he has abandoned the afflictions that are to be abandoned by seeing and meditation. If one asks, "Where was it taught?", therefore "Rājagṛha" is stated, because that specific city is called Rājagṛha. Because that city is extremely vast, there would be no certainty as to where he dwelt; therefore, "Vulture Peak Mountain" is stated, because the mass of that mountain resembles the shape of a vulture. "Monks", because they have destroyed all afflictions. "Community", because they cannot be divided by opponents; "great", because their number and qualities are great. "Bodhisattvas", because they establish themselves in unsurpassable awakening. "Great beings", because they place all sentient beings in the state of non-abiding nirvāṇa. "Dwelling" should be understood as dwelling through deportment. Through those, it is shown when, by whom, where, and to whom it was taught, clarifying the entities examined by realization. If one asks, "When?", it was taught at "one time." If one asks, "By whom?", by the "Bhagavān"; this indicates the excellence of the teacher. If one asks, "Where?", at "Rājagṛha, on Vulture Peak Mountain"; this indicates the excellence of the location. If one asks, "To whom?", to the "monks" and "bodhisattvas"; this indicates the excellence of the retinue. ^1-2

### The Absorption ^1-3-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-3]]

This is the absorption. If one did not enter absorption, one would not clearly realize the entities examined, so the absorption is stated in order to clearly realize the entities examined. "At that time" means exactly at the time when the Bhagavān was dwelling in Rājagṛha, and it is connected with entering absorption in samādhi and so forth. If one asks, "Into what named samādhi did he enter absorption?", therefore, "profound enumeration of phenomena" is stated. Herein, because they hold their own and general defining characteristics, they are "phenomena". The "enumeration" of those phenomena refers to the aggregates of phenomena, namely the five aggregates, the elements, and the sense sources. "Profound" refers to the meaning of emptiness by nature, because there is no arising and ceasing. Because, when one enters absorption in samādhi, one realizes that phenomena such as the aggregates are without arising and ceasing, the name of that samādhi is called "Profound Illumination of the Enumeration of Phenomena". "Illumination" refers to realization. "Entered absorption" means placing the mind continuously one-pointedly in samādhi. ^1-3

### Showing the Dialogue on the Training ^1-4-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-4]]

This indicates the raising of the introduction. If the introduction were not raised, the words would be confused and disconnected, so the raising of the introduction is stated in order to make the words connected and not confused. "Also at that time" refers to the very time when the Bhagavān entered absorption in Rājagṛha, and it is connected with the statement, "the noble Avalokiteśvara viewed the five aggregates as empty by nature." "Bodhi" refers to the expanse of the mind, suchness, the true limit. Striving and making effort in order to attain it is the "bodhisattva". "Noble", because he has abandoned the two obscurations far away. Regarding "Avalokita", because he looks upon all sentient beings with compassion in the manner of non-apprehension out of the three types of compassion, he is "Avalokita". "Īśvara", because he has found mastery in dispelling the suffering of sentient beings. Regarding "prajñā", there are three types of prajñā: because it cuts the net of the five aggregates, it is the sword of prajñā. Because it destroys the aggregate of suffering, it is also called the vajra of prajñā. Because it clears away the darkness of ignorance, it is also called the lamp of prajñā; in short, realizing all phenomena as empty through prajñā that is non-conceptual in all aspects is called "prajñā". Because that prajñā makes one go to the other shore, it is "pāramitā". There are three types of pāramitā: worldly pāramitā, supramundane pāramitā, and supramundane ultimate pāramitā. Herein, worldly pāramitā is abandoning the suffering of the three continuous paths. Supramundane pāramitā is, having abandoned the saṃsāra of birth and death, attaining one-sided peaceful nirvāṇa. Supramundane ultimate pāramitā: because the three realms are like a dream, one does not even desire nirvāṇa; because sentient beings are like an illusion, one does not even hope for the fruit of Buddhahood. Because all phenomena are nirvāṇa by nature, attaining non-abiding nirvāṇa is attaining ultimate pāramitā. If one asks, "What is that prajñāpāramitā?", therefore, "practicing the profound" is stated. "Profound" means being without arising and ceasing. "Practicing" refers to the meaning: being without arising and ceasing is called prajñāpāramitā. "Examining" means apprehending the meaning of being without arising and ceasing. ^1-4

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-5]]

Not only does one apprehend the meaning of being without arising and ceasing, but one also views the five aggregates, form and so forth, as empty. Herein, there are two types of emptiness: the emptiness of unconditioned space, and the emptiness of individual self-awareness free from apprehender and apprehended through noble pristine wisdom regarding the ultimate. The meaning is that one views the five aggregates as the emptiness free from apprehender and apprehended. There are three types of views: the view of ordinary beings and tīrthikas, the view of śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas, and the view of bodhisattvas and tathāgatas. Herein, ordinary beings and tīrthikas view the five aggregates as a life-force, a self, and a person. Śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas view the five aggregates as origination and suffering. Bodhisattvas and tathāgatas view and see the five aggregates as empty by nature. ^1-5

### Engaging in Prajñā ^1-5-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-6]]

This is engaging in prajñā. Because one does not know the method of engaging, the words of questioning are shown. If one asks what that is, it is "Then..." and so forth. If one asks, "Why did the noble Śāriputra not ask other bodhisattvas, but ask the bodhisattva Avalokiteśvara?" To that, this is said: when this prajñāpāramitā was explained, because that bodhisattva was the chief among the assembled retinue of countless hundreds of thousands, through the power of the Buddha, the noble Śāriputra, in order to dispel the doubts of himself and others, asked him the meanings occurring below. "Then" means after whatever was explained. "Through the power of the Buddha" means by the blessings of the tathāgatas. Regarding "Śāriputra", because he is designated with a name derived from the name of his mother, Śārikā, he is called Śāriputra. "Spoke these words" means he asked the meanings occurring below. ^1-6

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-7]]

"Whoever" means whoever it might be. Regarding "prajñāpāramitā": prajñā is of two kinds: the prajñā that knows the relative, and the prajñā that knows the ultimate. Herein, the prajñā that knows the relative is knowing all phenomena to be like an illusion, a mirage, and a dream. The prajñā that knows the ultimate is knowing all phenomena to be inexpressible and inconceivable like the surface of space; because that prajñā makes one go to the other shore of non-abiding nirvāṇa, it is called prajñāpāramitā. In fact, the profound meaning of being without arising and ceasing is called prajñāpāramitā. "The practitioner" is the person, the bodhisattva. "What is practiced" is the dharma, prajñāpāramitā. "If one asks, 'How should one train?'" means "Through listening, contemplating, and meditating, how should one engage in the meaning of prajñāpāramitā?" Alternatively, one should train in four types of practice. The four types of practice are: practicing the ultimate of non-conceptual pristine wisdom; practicing the dharmas on the side of awakening due to having no afflictions; practicing the complete ripening of sentient beings without clinging; and practicing the complete ripening of the Buddha-dharmas without attachment. "How should one train?" means how should one practice those four practices. ^1-7

### The Defining Characteristic of Prajñā ^1-6-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-8]]

This is the defining characteristic of prajñā. Because one cannot understand the meaning if one does not know the defining characteristic, it is shown by the words of reply to "bodhisattva mahāsattva" and so forth. "Spoke these words to Śāriputra" means he spoke these words occurring below. ^1-8

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-9]]

"Should examine in this way" means one should practice prajñāpāramitā in this way. "If one asks, 'How should one train?'", therefore: ^1-9

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-10]]

It is stated: "views those five aggregates also as empty by nature." The five aggregates are form, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness. Regarding "empty by nature": there are five types of emptiness: the emptiness of prior non-existence, the emptiness of non-existence after perishing, the emptiness of absolute non-existence, the emptiness of mutual non-existence, and the emptiness of no intrinsic nature. Herein, yogurt not existing in milk is the emptiness of prior non-existence. Milk not existing in yogurt is the emptiness of non-existence after perishing. Horns not existing on the lower parts of a rabbit's head is the emptiness of absolute non-existence. An ox not existing in a horse is the emptiness of mutual non-existence. All phenomena being without a nature is the emptiness of no intrinsic nature. Out of the five types of emptiness that exist, the meaning is that one should examine the five aggregates as empty with the emptiness of no intrinsic nature. ^1-10

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-11]]

Regarding "form is empty": "form" refers to earth, water, fire, and wind. "Empty" means that because the ultimate, the dharmadhātu, has the defining characteristic of emptiness, those forms are emptiness. That defining characteristic of emptiness is non-dual, beyond number and counting; because it has abandoned the views of self and what belongs to self, and is free from apprehended and apprehender, it is the defining characteristic of non-dual thusness. Because there is no phenomenon whatsoever that is not included in the defining characteristic of emptiness, the four of earth, water, fire, and wind also do not have a defining characteristic; they are without real entity, without a self, and without a principal; it is not that they become emptiness after being dismantled into particles, but because they are empty by intrinsic nature, it is said "form is empty". Whatever is the emptiness of form by intrinsic nature is precisely that ultimate emptiness; because there is no ultimate emptiness apart from form being empty by intrinsic nature, it is said "emptiness also is form," which means that the ultimate emptiness itself is the nature of the emptiness of form. If one asks, "What makes it evident that the emptiness of form is the ultimate emptiness?", from what is taught by the noble Akṣayamati: "The dharmadhātu of the pristine wisdom of the bodhisattva engaging in the dharmadhātu is the elements of earth, water, fire, and wind, yet the dharmadhātu does not have the defining characteristics of solidity, moisture, warmth, and motion. The dharmadhātu and all phenomena are equal." If one asks, "Why is that?", it is explained that they are equal because they are equal in emptiness. Therefore, one should know the emptiness of form to be the ultimate emptiness. Alternatively, regarding "form is empty": there are three types of form: thoroughly imputed form, conceptually constructed form, and the form of dharmatā. Herein, what ordinary childish beings impute as having the defining characteristic of solidity, such as earth and so forth, is thoroughly imputed form. Form that is the object of experience of consciousness possessing objects is conceptually constructed form. The defining characteristic of thusness, free from thoroughly imputed form and conceptually constructed form, is the form of dharmatā; because thoroughly imputed form and conceptually constructed form do not exist in that form of dharmatā, it is said "form is empty". Thinking, "Does that emptiness of the form of dharmatā exist separately from conceptually constructed form and completely imputed form?", it is stated "emptiness is form," which means that the emptiness of the form of dharmatā itself is of one defining characteristic with conceptually constructed and completely imputed form. Regarding "Whatever is form, that is emptiness; emptiness also is form; there is no emptiness apart from form": because the emptiness of the defining characteristic of form and ultimate emptiness are non-dual and one, it is said "there is no emptiness apart from form." If one asks, "Why are they non-dual?", it is because they are non-dual in the defining characteristic of the emptiness of intrinsic nature, which is free from addition and subtraction and has abandoned the two extremes. Regarding "Whatever is form is empty", it means that whatever is the defining characteristic of form is also the defining characteristic of emptiness. "Whatever is emptiness is form" means that whatever is the defining characteristic of emptiness is also the defining characteristic of form. By this, this is shown: lowly ordinary sentient beings who have been childish, since beginningless time, have cycled in the five paths within saṃsāra of birth and death; becoming familiar with the five aggregates and accustomed to the eighteen elements, they attach and cling to them. The Tathāgata demonstrated the things they apprehend as real entities and solid lumps to be empty by intrinsic nature. Thus, when the defining characteristic of form is dismantled into emptiness, apprehending the single antidote of emptiness to exist separately is the śrāvakas' attachment to the one-sided nirvāṇa of pacification. Thus: "Whatever is form is emptiness" was spoken as the antidote to separate from falling into the extreme of saṃsāra due to attachment to form. "Whatever is emptiness is form" was spoken as the antidote for the śrāvakas who, having realized the selflessness of persons and dismantled form into emptiness, subsequently generate the mark of emptiness and fall into the extreme of nirvāṇa. ^1-11

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-12]]

If form and emptiness are to be abandoned because they are non-existent, then in that regard, if emptiness is taught in order to abandon the apprehension of form as a mark, since a mind apprehending emptiness as a mark arises, both emptiness and form are subsequently abandoned. As for marks, because focusing on any mark whatsoever obscures the realization of reality itself, apprehending emptiness as a real entity is also a mark. To give an example for this: a person with impaired vision is traveling on a journey; on the right side of the path, there are thorns and pits. On the left side of the path, there are ravines and precipices. If a person with flawless vision says, 'There are thorns and pits,' he falls into the extreme of the ravines and precipices. If he says, 'There are ravines and precipices,' he falls into the extreme of the thorns and pits. Instead, by demonstrating the comfortable middle path that is unobstructed by anything, he brings him to his own destination. Similar to that example, 'the one with impaired vision' refers to ordinary beings obscured by the afflictive obscurations and śrāvakas obscured by the cognitive obscurations. ^1-12

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-13]]

The thorns and pits are individuals' attachment to the marks of form and so forth, which is falling into the extreme of saṃsāra. The ravines and precipices are the śrāvakas' attachment to nirvāṇa, which is falling into the extreme of emptiness. The person with vision is the Tathāgata; because he sees with the clear eye of prajñā that form is empty by intrinsic nature, he does not abandon saṃsāra, since birth and death are like an illusion. Since the three realms are like a dream, he also does not seek the qualities of nirvāṇa. Having entered the middle path, the path of the signless, the wishless, and emptiness, he reaches the destination of non-abiding nirvāṇa. Therefore, in order to demonstrate that apprehending as a mark is a precipice and a fault, one should not apprehend either existence or non-existence as a mark. ^1-13

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-14]]

Likewise, regarding the statement 'feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness are empty', it is said: Above, regarding the statement 'to see the five aggregates as empty', the emptiness of form was mentioned. The phrases mentioning that form is taught to be empty and that emptiness and form are taught to be undifferentiated, when combined with the three types of explanation, become six types. The three types of explanation are: first, they are explained as identical because they are empty by intrinsic nature; [second], they are explained as having one taste by combining them with the three types of designation; and finally, having abandoned both ordinary beings of saṃsāra who abide in the extreme of real entities, and śrāvakas who abide in the extreme of the emptiness of nirvāṇa, entering the middle path is established. Just as each of these three, when combined with the respective pairs 'form is empty' and 'emptiness is form', becomes six, likewise, this method is also explained for the five or four aggregates. Just as they are explained by the five types of mutual emptiness, similarly, the remaining feeling, perception, formation, and consciousness should also be explained by the five types of mutual emptiness. These dharmas are the continuum of one's own mind, and the mind, being formless and having the defining characteristic of emptiness, ripens its latencies and depends on that aggregate of form. That is, for example, like an empty vessel; it depends on a vessel. If that vessel is destroyed, there is no basis of dependence, and it is undifferentiated from the great emptiness. Similar to that, when this aggregate of form is analyzed as empty, that aggregate of mind also has no basis of abiding, and is undifferentiated from the ultimate dharmadhātu. If one asks, 'How is it evident that the five aggregates are empty by intrinsic nature?', this is explained in the teaching of the Noble Akṣayamati: 'The aggregate of form is like a mass of foam; it cannot withstand being grasped or cut. The aggregate of feeling is like a water bubble; it is momentary and impermanent. The aggregate of perception is like a mirage; because out of thirsty craving, one apprehends what is mistaken. The aggregate of formation is like a water-born plant; when taken apart, it is essenceless. The aggregate of consciousness is like a dream; it is an erroneous apprehension.' Therefore, the five aggregates are not a self, not an individual, not a sentient being, not a life, not a nourisher, not a person. By the intrinsic nature of the five aggregates, they are not like this; rather, by the intrinsic nature of the five aggregates, they are like this: empty of self and what belongs to self; unarisen, unproduced, non-existent, the expanse of space, unconditioned, and by intrinsic nature passed into nirvāṇa. Thus, because the four great external elements are known as empty, form is empty. Because the four internal [aggregates of] mind are known as empty, this is called 'emptiness of emptiness'. By abandoning the mark of form and emptiness being identical, and being free from the duality of apprehended and apprehender, it is called the 'body of complete liberation'. ^1-14

### The Sphere of Activity of Prajñā ^1-7-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-15]]

This is the domain of prajñā; since it is unsuitable to meditate if one does not know the domain, it is explained in order to establish one in yoga meditation. As for the statement 'Śāriputra, thus', it is as explained above. Regarding the statement 'all dharmas are emptiness': 'all dharmas' relies on the five aggregates, and refers to the five faculties, objects, elements, sense-sources, dependent origination, and so forth. By realizing those five aggregates as empty, their branches should also be known as having the defining characteristic of emptiness. For example, by realizing the main body as empty, the limbs and so forth should concomitantly be known. ^1-15

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-16]]

Regarding the statement 'without defining characteristics': because all dharmas transcend marks and what possesses marks, they are called 'without defining characteristics'. ^1-16

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-17]]

Regarding the statement 'unarisen, unceasing': existing later from not existing previously is called 'arisen'. Becoming non-existent later from existing previously is called 'ceased'. Because this ultimate emptiness, the dharmadhātu, the intrinsic nature of a Buddha, has no beginning limit, an end limit is not found. Therefore, it is called 'unarisen and unceasing'. ^1-17

Even when sentient beings wander in saṃsāra within the five transmigratory paths, because the intrinsic nature of a Buddha does not become stained, it is called 'completely pure'. 'not body,' ^1-31

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-32]]

Regarding 'not mind', concerning this it is said: These are the six faculties. Since the six faculties depend on the five aggregates, if the five aggregates do not exist, the six faculties are also said to be non-existent, because they are not other than the five aggregates. ^1-32

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-33]]

'not form,' ^1-33

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-34]]

'not sound,' ^1-34

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-35]]

'not smell,' ^1-35

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-36]]

'not taste,' ^1-36

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-37]]

'not touch,' ^1-37

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-38]]

Regarding 'not phenomena', this is what is said: These are the six objects. Through the power of ignorance, latent tendencies ripen, and a corrupted mind grasps as existing what is established as the results of conditioned things; but ultimately they are said to be non-existent because they are empty of intrinsic nature. ^1-38

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-39]]

Regarding 'not eye element up to not mind element', this is what is said: these are the eighteen elements; because the six internal sense faculties are non-existent, there is no support for the six consciousnesses, ^1-39

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-40]]

and because the six external objects are non-existent, there is no basis for the six consciousnesses; since they do not arise on their own, the six consciousnesses are also non-existent; therefore, it is said that the eighteen elements are also non-existent. ^1-40

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-41]]

'not ignorance,' ^1-41

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-42]]

'not extinction of ignorance, not aging and death,' ^1-42

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-43]]

Regarding 'not extinction of aging and death', this is what is said: This is the domain of practice of those who have entered the vehicle of the pratyekabuddhas. To state from ignorance up to aging and death is to speak in summary; from formations by the condition of ignorance, one should view up to consciousness, name and form, the six sense sources, contact, feeling, craving, grasping, existence, birth, and aging and death. 'Ignorance' means that karma arises through the view of self. Grasping at a self arises from non-entity; therefore, ignorance is also a non-entity. For example, if a house has remained in darkness for a long time, the appearance of a single lamp makes it instantly non-existent, and that non-existent entity is also not seen. Similarly, the dense darkness of sentient beings' ignorance from beginningless time is made instantly non-existent when examined with the lamp of prajñā, and that non-existent entity is also not seen. Moreover, 'awareness' is merely imputed as the counterpart of ignorance; since ignorance is non-existent, the name of awareness is also not established, and therefore it is said 'not ignorance, not extinction of ignorance'. Because the entity of ignorance itself is non-existent, ignorance is non-existent. Because it does not appear like a non-existent entity, extinction is also said to be non-existent. ^1-43

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-44]]

Regarding 'not suffering, origin, cessation, and path', this is what is said: This is the domain of practice of those who have entered the vehicle of the śrāvakas, which is called the four truths. Truths have three aspects: mundane, supramundane, and ultimate supramundane truths. Among these, mundane truth is knowing the five aggregates as the origin and as the suffering of aging and death. Supramundane truth is knowing suffering, origin, cessation, and path. Supramundane noble truth is knowing the five aggregates as having no birth, which is knowing the truth of suffering. Whatever conquers existence is knowing the truth of origin. Knowing ignorance and latent tendencies as non-existent by intrinsic nature is knowing the truth of cessation. By understanding phenomena as equality, not superimposing upon all things is called knowing the truth of the path. Thus, because the truths also ultimately do not exist by intrinsic nature, they are said to be 'not'. ^1-44

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-45]]

If one asks, 'How is it evident that the truths are non-existent by intrinsic nature?', it is explained in the noble Akṣayamatinirdeśasūtra: 'What makes a bodhisattva skilled in the truths? These aggregates are suffering; whoever realizes the mark of suffering as emptiness just as it is, that is called the noble truth of suffering. The causes of craving and the causes of views concerning the five aggregates are the origin; whoever does not apprehend and does not superimpose those causes of craving and causes of views, that is called the noble truth of origin. Knowing that the aggregates do not arise in the past limit, do not transfer to the future limit, and do not remain in the present is called the noble truth of cessation. Whoever, resting evenly in non-dual pristine wisdom, knows the four truths as emptiness, that is explained as the noble truth of the path.' ^1-45

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-46]]

Regarding 'not pristine wisdom, not attainment, and also not non-attainment', concerning this, this is what is said: This is the domain of practice of those who have entered the great vehicle of the bodhisattvas. That pristine wisdom of abandoning the two, the afflictive obscuration and the cognitive obscuration, is called non-dual pristine wisdom; because ultimately it is not pristine wisdom, that pristine wisdom is said to be non-existent. ^1-46

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-47]]

'Attainment' means attaining from what was previously non-existent. Because if there is attainment, there is also emptiness, and if there is a result, it is subject to destruction, the intrinsic nature of a Buddha abides equally in all sentient beings; it is empty in the beginning and there is no attainment in the end. The mention of the sequence of the ten grounds is also merely the gradual purification of the latent tendencies of ignorance in the all-ground consciousness; once the latent tendencies of ignorance are purified, within the dharmadhātu, which is like the mirror of a Buddha's pristine wisdom, there is no imputing 'attainment' and 'non-attainment', and therefore attainment is also non-existent. ^1-47

### The Qualities of Prajñāpāramitā ^1-8-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-48]]

Non-attainment is also said to be non-existent. Therefore, it is also said in the Prajñāpāramitā in Seven Hundred Lines: 'Not engaging in any marks is engaging in prajñāpāramitā. Attaining nothing at all is attaining unsurpassable awakening.' These are the qualities of prajñāpāramitā. Although a yogi's unsurpassable awakening is the nature of emptiness, nevertheless, the result of the perfection is also not non-existent; the qualities are explained to generate enthusiasm, saying 'It exists'. Regarding 'Śāriputra, therefore, because bodhisattvas have no attainment, they abide in and rely on prajñāpāramitā', concerning this, this is what is said: 'Therefore' is as explained above. 'Because there is no attainment' is because there is no attaining the result of unsurpassable, perfectly complete awakening. ^1-48

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-49]]

'Abiding in and relying on prajñāpāramitā' means not abiding in any marks. ^1-49

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-50]]

'Having no obscuration of mind' is because the inner latent tendencies and all outer marks have been abandoned and are non-existent. Regarding 'having no fear', because there is thus no obscuration of mind, it is because there is no fear of being born in saṃsāra and the lower realms, and no fear regarding the meaning of profound phenomena having no birth and no cessation. ^1-50

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-51]]

Regarding 'completely passed beyond inverted views', viewing the non-existent outer objects of the latent tendencies of ignorance in the all-ground consciousness as various aspects is called 'inverted views'; by understanding the selflessness of persons and phenomena regarding this, and abandoning the afflictive and cognitive obscurations, one passes beyond inverted views. ^1-51

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-52]]

Regarding 'attaining nirvāṇa', ignorance is the ground of all afflictions, and because it becomes the cause of saṃsāra even within the twelve links of dependent origination, it is said that 'finally an entire mass of suffering is assembled.' If one knows ignorance to be non-existent, suffering and all afflictions also become non-existent. Therefore, it is called 'attaining nirvāṇa'. ^1-52

### The Result of Prajñāpāramitā ^1-9-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-53]]

This is the result of prajñāpāramitā; because if there were no result, the practice would also be meaningless, the result is explained. ^1-53

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-54]]

Regarding 'all Buddhas who abide in the three times, having relied on prajñāpāramitā', concerning this, this is what is said: 'Times' refers to the distinct times of the past, future, and present. 'Abide' refers to those who have gone in the three times. 'Buddhas' are called 'buddhas' because they have attained the eye of prajñā out from the corruption of saṃsāra of the three realms; in reality, having become the dharmakāya, the perfection of innumerable qualities—such as prajñā, pristine wisdom, samādhi, the major marks, the minor signs, and the unshared qualities—is called a 'buddha'. 'Relying on prajñāpāramitā' is because of abiding in the absence of all marks. Because all buddhas of the three times equally arise from prajñāpāramitā, this prajñāpāramitā is called the mother of all buddhas. ^1-54

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-55]]

Regarding 'fully awaken to unsurpassable, perfectly complete awakening', concerning this, this is what is said: 'Unsurpassable' means having passed beyond the three realms. 'Perfectly complete' is the ultimate limit of phenomena, the unerring suchness. 'Awakening' is suchness, the dharmadhātu. 'Fully awaken' refers to having practiced prajñāpāramitā in a non-conceptual manner and having manifestly awakened, being no different from the buddhas of the past and being equal in qualities. ^1-55

### The Dhāraṇī of Prajñā ^1-10-0

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-56]]

This is the dhāraṇī of prajñā; it is stated in order not to fall. Therefore, regarding 'true and unerring', concerning this, this is what is said: Because there is no speaking with words, there is wishlessness; thus that is the truth of speech. Because there is no conduct of the body, there is signlessness; thus it is the truth of the body. Because there is no thinking of the mind, from emptiness it is the truth of the mind. Thus, it does not contradict the three gateways to noble liberation; and because prajñāpāramitā, having cleared away all marks of the mind, accords with the meaning of non-conceptuality, the gateway of simultaneous entry into the ultimate, it is true and unerring. Because it clears away all inner latent tendencies of consciousness, it is the mantra of prajñāpāramitā. ^1-56

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-57]]

Because it knows and clears away all outer marks by their very nature, it is the mantra of great knowledge. ^1-57

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-58]]

Because it clears away all marks of both outer and inner, it is the unsurpassable mantra. ^1-58

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-59]]

Because it accomplishes the result of buddhahood, it is the mantra equal to the unequalled. ^1-59

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-60]]

Because it abandons all lower realms and accomplishes the benefit of sentient beings, it is the mantra that completely pacifies all suffering. ^1-60

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-61]]

Regarding 'should be known', concerning this, this is what is said: This means it should be known that this prajñāpāramitā is the cause that makes one attain buddhahood. ^1-61

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-62]]

The mantra of prajñāpāramitā is spoken: ^1-62

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-63]]

Tadyathā: gate gate pāragate pārasaṃgate bodhi svāhā. ^1-63

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-64]]

This mantra of prajñāpāramitā becomes the cause of mundane and supramundane merit. Mundane merit means that one is unharmed by afflictions, māras, and obstructors, and is able to be protected. Supramundane merit is that, by relying on this dhāraṇī, it becomes the cause of prajñā and jñāna. ^1-64

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-65]]

Regarding 'Then the Bhagavan also arose from that samādhi', concerning this, this is what is said: Except for the Tathāgata alone, others are not able to explain and listen to the meaning of prajñāpāramitā; therefore, noble Avalokiteśvara explaining and Śāriputra listening were also the blessing of that samādhi. Since the actions of explaining and listening had been done, he arose from that samādhi. ^1-65

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-66]]

Having seen the complete purity of the cause and the complete purity of the result, the Bhagavan gave his approval 'well done' to noble Avalokiteśvara. The Buddha, having seen the complete purity of the cause and result, gave his approval 'well done', and then ^1-66

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-67]]

the gods, humans, and so forth also ^1-67

![[bo-prasastrasena-tika#^1-68]]

manifestly rejoiced and praised what the Bhagavan had spoken. ^1-68

The Extensive Commentary on the Heart of Prajñāpāramitā composed by Praśāstrasena is complete. ^1-69
