# Extensive Explanation of the Prajñāpāramitā Heart Sūtra by Mahāpaṇḍita Vimalamitra ^0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^0-1]]

The Extensive Commentary on the Noble Heart of Prajñāpāramitā ^0-1

## Prompting the Discourse ^1-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^1-1]]

In the language of India: Āryaprajñāpāramitāhṛdayaṭīkā. ^1-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^1-2]]

In the Tibetan language: The Extensive Commentary on the Noble Heart of Prajñāpāramitā. ^1-2

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^1-3]]

Homage to the bodhisattva mahāsattva, the noble Avalokiteśvara, who is endowed with great compassion! Having paid homage to all the victors of the three times along with the bodhisattvas, the sole friends of migrating beings, I shall explain the heart of the mother of the victors, the supreme principal meaning of the One Hundred Thousand and so forth. The introduction, the setting, what is included in the teaching, the assembly, the basis of the discourse, the question, and the reply—which has eleven aspects—and the corresponding rejoicing: these are summarised into eight aspects. Here, being exhorted by the retinue, the compiler, ^1-3

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^1-4]]

having briefly summarised the entity to be taught, initiates the discourse with the first three words, "Thus" and so forth. What I heard is "thus"; it is not otherwise. By this, in order to abandon subtraction and addition, one pledges the exact authenticity of what was heard. This expresses that, having heard it once, taking it up and retaining it are perfectly demonstrated. Therefore, it should be known that the compiler has engendered roots of virtue with regard to the Buddha, has fully ripened them, is endowed with a protector who is a spiritual friend, has venerated and perfectly questioned the Buddha, and has practiced generosity, ethical discipline, and so forth. Otherwise, it would be impossible to hear and so forth the principal meaning of such a prajñāpāramitā. As the protector Maitreya has said: "Those who have rendered exceptional service to the Buddhas, engendered roots of virtue toward them, and are endowed with a protector who is a spiritual friend, become vessels for hearing this. Because they have venerated the Buddha, asked many questions, and practiced generosity, ethical discipline, and so forth, the wise know them to be vessels for taking it up, retaining it, and so forth." Those also are in order to engender faith, undistractedness, reverence, and so forth in those to be tamed. The occurrence of "by me" in the third case indicates that the exact sound of the letters was heard; therefore, knowledge arisen from hearing completely excludes the entity of one's own realisation. Otherwise, "heard by me" would become the sixth case, because it would have the meaning of knowledge. ^1-4

## Composition ^2-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^2-1]]

Now, in order to demonstrate the intrinsic nature of what was heard, the setting begins with "at one time" and so forth. "Time" is a time such as autumn. The occurrence of "one" specifies this occurrence of "time." And that is to be connected with the very occurrence of "was dwelling." Because he has vanquished the four māras, he is the Bhagavān; although he acts as the guru of migrating beings who has become supreme and infinite in qualities and enlightened activities, and although he possesses infinite names through his qualities, nevertheless, in order to demonstrate to the retinue of the three vehicles engaged in the welfare of self and others that he is simultaneously worthy of being a refuge, the reason for applying the word "Bhagavān" is demonstrated as being precisely the eradication of the causes of fear. Therefore, in all contexts of explanation, the word "Bhagavān" is uttered. The occurrence of "Rājagṛha" is a specific city in the region of Magadha. In one area of it is the mountain called Vulture Peak, which is the constant dwelling place of the Buddha, so he was dwelling there. Vulture Peak mountain became a stūpa through the Bhagavān dwelling there, and it should be known as being indestructible even by destructive forces such as fire, because it is stated thus in the Bodhisattvapiṭaka. Likewise, it is also in accord with occurrences in the noble Candrapradīpa and so forth: "In this very eastern direction of Rājagṛha, one saw thousands of tens of millions of Buddhas, and in the presence of all victors, requested this supreme peaceful samādhi," and so forth. How could the direction where the Tathāgata—who is fully liberated from all flaws such as conceptuality and analysis—dwells in any of the four postures, which has arisen by the power of the Tathāgata, be destroyed by destructive forces? Because the excellent dwellings of deities, noble ones, and Brahmā—the fourth dhyāna, emptiness, and great compassion respectively—are the principal characteristics, he was dwelling through those three dwellings. The word for that has the meaning of having already dwelled. The Bhagavān dwelling there is in the past at the time of perfectly compiling the Dharma. By the specification of the occurrence "at one time," it is demonstrated that the guru of migrating beings, who is dedicated to great compassion, dwells in other regions at other times. Therefore, other followers of the Bhagavān should also abide through the three excellent dwellings, but not through just any activities. ^2-1

## Showing the Summary of the Teaching ^3-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^3-1]]

The occurrence of "and a great saṅgha of monks" and so forth demonstrates the gathering of those belonging to the teaching. Herein, because they have destroyed the afflictions, they are monks. Because they are gathered in a multitude and are indivisible, they are a saṅgha. The occurrence of "great" is either because they are a vast majority, or because of their prajñā, miraculous powers, and so forth. Likewise, "and a great saṅgha of bodhisattva mahāsattvas" as well; here too, their greatness is demonstrated in three ways: by greatness of mind having become supreme among all sentient beings, by greatness of abandonment, and by greatness of realisation. "Together with them" has the meaning of "along with." Because they are together with regard to the action of dwelling, it is the third case through the characteristic of the meaning of "along with." With regard to this "gathering of those belonging," if one asks who belongs: those belonging are those who have fully ripened the previously engendered roots of virtue that will accomplish the appearance of hearing and so forth such a Dharma, which is to say they have the fortune for hearing the Dharma and so forth. I have not understood what some great ones say: "In order to establish oneself as valid, one takes the place, time, and retinue as witnesses, and if one speaks with witnesses, the speaker will be understood to be valid;" if one were to go to those places in this way and ask the witnesses, ascertaining the meaning of the sūtra would be a remote prospect for those without that ability. Furthermore, just as in this very sūtra, how could one speak of "witnesses" where the names of the retinue are not demonstrated? Because they are lower, the retinue of monks is demonstrated first, and because they are higher, the retinue of bodhisattvas is demonstrated later; just as in "whatever merit of migrating beings of the ten directions, learners, non-learners, and pratyekabuddhas," and so forth, in some cases they are arranged sequentially from the lower. In some cases they are arranged sequentially from the higher, just as in "Buddhas and bodhisattvas." Others say: "Because they are a constant retinue, the retinue of monks is mentioned first; because they are not constant, the retinue of bodhisattvas is mentioned later." To say "the constant ones are to be expressed first and the others later" is without logic. ^3-1

## The Setting ^4-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^4-1]]

In order to explain the setting by which this teaching is entered into, "At that time" and so forth is spoken. It is called "at that time" because it is the time when the roots of virtue of the retinue are demonstrated to be fully ripened, and because it is the time the Bhagavan enters evenly into samādhi; for the Bhagavan knows the time. "Phenomena" are the aggregates and so forth, as they are asserted to precisely hold their own characteristics. Because one understands, realises, and knows completely, totally, and entirely with regard to this or by means of this, it is called an "enumeration of phenomena." "Profound" is emptiness and so forth. Where or by what it illuminates in relation to one's own and others' continuums respectively, that is "profound illumination." Accordingly, it is taught: "The profound is emptiness and so forth; being free from the extremes of exaggeration and deprecation, that is the profound itself." Moreover, being free from the extremes of exaggeration and deprecation is passing beyond existence and non-existence. Therefore, from the presence of Master Nāgārjuna it is taught: "Those whose minds have fully transcended and do not dwell upon existence and non-existence fully cultivate the meaning through the condition of the objectless profound." "Samādhi" has the characteristic of one-pointedness of mind. Because of placing perfectly, entering into its nature is "entering evenly." Thus, all the deeds of Buddhas and bodhisattvas have the benefit of others as their primary focus. Therefore, the Bhagavan entering evenly into this samādhi generated what is suitable to fully ripen their respective continuums in whatever way accords with demonstrating the meaning of the samādhi to the mindstreams of all the retinue; that is to be understood in that way. ^4-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^4-2]]

Here, the Bhagavan's non-afflicted knowledge is also posited as the source: the non-affliction of the afflicted ones means that when seeing me, people's afflictions are completely abandoned. The non-affliction of the Jinas is that they cut off the continuum of their afflictions in cities and so forth. Because this very meaning is demonstrated below, "And at that time" and so forth is spoken. "At that time" means at that time the Bhagavan entered evenly into samādhi. The word "and" has the meaning of definite grasping; therefore, it means "that very same." Alternatively, "and" is for the purpose of collecting: it gathers together the demonstration of the action of entering evenly and the action of observing that occurs below. In this explanation, only the time of the full ripening of the retinue's roots of virtue is to be fully apprehended; if so, the collecting is feasible, but otherwise it is not. The "sattva" of this is for the sake of bodhi, or because the mind is bodhi itself, it is a bodhisattva. Because they have entered into Buddhahood itself, for those who have attained empowerment the latter etymology is logical. Furthermore, if one asks what this "bodhi" is: Bodhi has the characteristic of space, because it has abandoned all concepts. Accordingly, it is also spoken in the Mahāvairocanābhisambodhi: "Bodhi is the characteristic of space; it is the abandonment of all concepts." Whoever desires to realise that is a bodhisattva; to them it is said like that. Because their "sattva" is great by way of the three—greatness of mind having become supreme among all sentient beings, and so forth, which were demonstrated—and by way of the greatness of the pāramitā of entering into the great grounds, they are a "mahāsattva". Accordingly, it is spoken in the Saṃcayagāthā: "Great giving, great intelligence, great power, entering into the supreme Mahāyāna of the Jinas, donning the great armour, and taming the illusions of māra; because of that, they are perfectly expressed as mahāsattvas." Because they have gone far away from the phenomena of non-virtuous misdeeds, they are "noble". If they look with their eyes, sentient beings have mastery; when they come to have mastery without relying on the causes of fear and suffering, to that master it is said like that. Because they are both noble and Avalokiteśvara, they are Ārya Avalokiteśvara. Because they know various aspects, or are supreme in knowing, they have prajñā. The entity of the supreme is supremacy itself, so it is just the supreme. That than which there is no other supreme in the world is explained as supreme; the supremacy of prajñā is the prajñāpāramitā. Therefore, it is said in this way: "Knowing by way of aspects such as the emptiness of own-essence becomes supreme; whatever is endowed with the absolute supreme is the prajñāpāramitā itself." Accordingly, it is spoken in the Noble Aṣṭasāhasrikā-prajñāpāramitā: "Ānanda, if one fully dedicates the roots of virtue to unsurpassed perfectly complete bodhi with whatever prajñā, is that supreme?" Ānanda replied, "Bhagavan, it is supreme. Sugata, it is supreme." The Bhagavan said, "Therefore, because it is supreme, it obtains the name pāramitā." For anything other than that, the etymology is not logical, applying the words is also difficult, and it would also contradict the Bhagavan's etymology. ^4-2

## The Eleven Aspects ^5-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^5-1]]

The ultimate prajñāpāramitā is the dharmakāya, which has the characteristic of being completely pure in all aspects. For what is other than that, it has the nature of the cause, which has the characteristics of the accomplishments of generating bodhicitta, armour, engaging, accumulation, and definite emergence. The meaning of "profound" has already been explained previously. Here, when applied to the causal prajñāpāramitā, it is the seventh case of location. When applied to the fruition, at that time it is the seventh case of reason. Regarding that, "practice" has many aspects: on the path of preparation on the ground of ordinary beings, it is the practice of aspiration. On the six grounds starting with the second ground, it is the practice of accomplishment. On the eighth ground, it is the definite practice. Among these, the practice of aspiration has three aspects: one's own purpose, one's own and others' purpose, and others' purpose; by dividing each of these separately into the smallest of the small and so forth, there are nine aspects for each. Thus, there are twenty-seven aspects of aspiration. The practice of accomplishment is the entity of the complete purity of the three trainings, exceptional discipline and so forth; among those, by way of the complete purity of the training of exceptional discipline, it is the second ground. By way of the complete purity of the training of exceptional mind, it is the third ground. By way of the complete purity of the training of exceptional prajñā, there are eight aspects; by that, the remaining eight grounds are gathered. Regarding that, by way of the complete purity of the training of exceptional prajñā endowed with abiding without marks along with effort, dependent origination, the four noble truths, and the factors of bodhi, it is the four grounds such as the Radiant; thus, the six aspects are the practice of accomplishment. The definite practice is the eighth ground by way of the complete purity of the training of exceptional prajñā that is spontaneous and signless; because practicing upon completely pure buddha-fields however one desires is definite, it is expressed as definite practice. Here, because no particularity is apprehended, all practices are apprehended. "Practicing" means practice: it is accomplishing subsequently. Alternatively, aspiration itself is the practice, because this is the ground of undertaking. Because by this itself, the eleven grounds such as the Joyous are undertaken. Attainment, perfect accomplishment, and complete purity are posited by the other remaining grounds. "Observing" the very practicing towards that object or for the sake of that means: they had engaged precisely in conformity with the Bhagavan's actions, and the meaning is that they do not perform any other action. Even though other retinues have also engaged following the actions of the Tathāgata, nevertheless, because Ārya Avalokiteśvara is the explainer, it is demonstrated that they engaged in this way. And because Śāriputra is the questioner. What occurs as "observing" means observing in various aspects such as the emptiness of own-essence; it is individual investigation. When it occurs as "in this way" in some texts, at that time, by this "in this way", it asserts a distinguished observation by way of the aspects occurring below, such as the emptiness of own-entity, signlessness, and non-arising. For that very reason, the expression of the meaning of "various" is applied to the word "rnam par". ^5-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^5-2]]

In order to demonstrate the meaning of however many things there are to be observed, it is stated, "the five aggregates." By the word "aggregates," the sense sources and elements are also indicated, because when concluding, those are also expressed. Alternatively, by the word "also," the sense sources and elements are also observed. By what occurs as "those also are empty of own-essence," it demonstrates existing just as it is; the meaning is empty of own-essence, devoid, and selfless. This also indicates signlessness, non-arising, and so forth. ^5-2

## The Question ^6-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^6-1]]

What occurs as "then" means immediately after in this context. The power of the Buddha's action is "by the Buddha's power": the words mean by the blessing and power of the Bhagavan's action; the Bhagavan accomplished the eloquence to ask this meaning of Ārya Avalokiteśvara. Otherwise, it is demonstrated that this would not be Śāriputra's domain. Because he possesses life by way of being worthy of praise due to abandoning the afflictions, he is "venerable." "Śāri's" is a woman born in the family of Śaradvata; because he is her son, he is "Śāriputra." What occurs as "these words" is stated below. What occurs as "said" is what will be said. ^6-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^6-2]]

What occurs as "son of noble family" is for the purpose of praising: because by the very obtainment of empowerment he has become an exceptional son of the buddhas, this is a vocative. What occurs as "whoever" does not demonstrate a particularity. What occurs as "to practice" is performing the practice and perfectly accomplishing with earnestness. "Wishing" is the aspiration for this; to that, it is spoken thus. In some texts it occurs as "by that one who wishes to perform the practice"; the meaning is the very lack of difference between "performing" and "accomplishing." By whatever aspects of intent, application, actualization, accomplishment, and realization, "how should they train?" They should habituate themselves. ^6-2

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^6-3]]

What occurs as "thus" applies to the previous aspect. What occurs as "having said" means "having expressed." ^6-3

## The Answer ^7-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-1]]

What occurs as "son of noble family or daughter of noble family" is worthy to be expressed by noble ones. The word that occurs as "or" is mutually dependent, so it should apply to both. What occurs as "by that one" is by the one wishing to perform the practice. What occurs as "thus" is by way of what occurs below. What occurs as "should observe" is to individually investigate in various aspects. By this, the object of special insight, the image with conceptualization, is expressed. Uninterruptedly from that very mind, the object of calm abiding as well, the image entirely without conceptualization, should be observed. ^7-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-2]]

What occurs as "perfectly continuously observe" means "perfectly" because it is unmistaken; or "evenly" because it is indivisible due to the very signlessness; or because of all phenomena, thoroughly observing and observing in accordance is perfectly continuously observing; the words mean actualizing the object of the limit of entities through calm abiding and special insight. By this, the object of the limit of entities demonstrates the first ground, which is subsumed by the path of seeing. Then, higher up, there is the limit of entities and the two aspects previously demonstrated; for the second ground and so forth, it should be observed that they are those three objects. On the tenth ground, which reaches the limit, and the tathāgata ground, because of obtaining the dharma body, the object is that very completely accomplished purpose. Thus, from the Noble Saṃdhinirmocana: "Previously, because of obtaining calm abiding and special insight, the object of the image with conceptualization and without conceptualization is obtained. Now, because of obtaining the path of seeing, the object of the limit of entities is obtained. By that, when mentally engaging in those very three objects, which are thoroughly endowed with the path of meditation higher and higher, because all signs and bad states are destroyed, gradually purifying the mind higher and higher on the grounds like gold, up to manifestly completely awakening in unsurpassed perfectly complete awakening, the object of the completely accomplished purpose is also obtained." Thus it is stated. In detail, it should be known from that very text. Here, only the application of the example is stated. ^7-2

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-3]]

If one asks, "How is observing the aggregates and so forth by way of aspects such as being empty of own entity 'perfect' because of being unmistaken, 'even' because of having a single characteristic due to the very signlessness, and perfectly continuously seeing all phenomena?" it is stated, "Form is empty." Because it is the context, it should be connected with what occurs as "of own-essence." Thus, regarding what appears as form and so forth, those that appear to be entities and those that appear to be imputed are realized in those two aspects. Regarding that, those who propound that appearances of form and so forth are entities are the Vaibhāṣikas, Mīmāṃsakas, and so forth. Those who propound that appearing objects are imputed are the Sautrāntikas; they state thus: "Objects, blue and so forth, exist externally, and experience is whatever aspect is cultivated in this consciousness." Regarding blue and so forth, the many conceptualizations of own-essence—infinitesimal particles, that which is composed of them having parts, consciousness, the primal substance, the Brahma of sound, and so forth—all those bad webs of conceptualizations of various transformations will be destroyed by the very refutation of own-essence, therefore that very thing should be expressed. Whatever among those is renowned even down to cowherds and women, that very form that appears clearly and directly is empty of own-essence like a city of gandharvas; in this appearing form there is no own-essence at all. ^7-3

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-4]]

Therefore, this very emptiness of own-essence is called "form"; by this, the form asserted by the Vaibhāṣikas, Mīmāṃsakas, and so forth is demonstrated to be the emptiness of own-essence. For the Sautrāntikas, because appearances are the essence of consciousness, what appears as blue and so forth is empty of the own-essence of objects. ^7-4

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-5]]

If they think, "Because external objects exclusively exist, form is other than emptiness," for those as well, "emptiness is not other than form." ^7-5

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-6]]

What occurs as "form also is not other than emptiness" refutes this. Moreover, if one asks thus: "From what should it be known that the aggregates and so forth have no own-essence?" Here it is stated: Because the aggregates, elements, and sense sources are dependently originated, the positions of being without cause and of being permanent are eliminated. Regarding the position of arising from causes as well, there are two alternatives for permanence: when an entity arises, does it arise at exactly the same time as the cause, or at a different time? Regarding that, if according to the first alternative, it is because all aspects of earlier and later causes and results would be observed at a single time, and cause and result would be indivisible, and an eon as well would become merely a single year. Regarding the second alternative as well, because the cause and result do not assemble at a single time, there is no potency; therefore, it would absurdly follow that they are without cause. If there is no cause, it would absurdly follow that they are permanently existent or non-existent, because there is no other to be dependent upon. If they are dependent, entities would become occasional. A third alternative is impossible, because the two—being at the same time and being at different times—abide mutually excluding each other. Therefore, whatever appearances of form and so forth there are, they are empty of own-essence like the water of a mirage and so forth. For them as well, whatever conceptualization there is, saying "it arises depending on this and that," is the conventional. For that very reason, the Bhagavān stated in the Noble Lalitavistara: "The son of the Śākyas, having seen dependently originated phenomena / to be without own-essence, / thoroughly endowed with a mind like space, / is unmoving upon seeing the deceitful one with his army." Thus it occurs, and from the Ratnolkā as well: "What is born from conditions is born in the house of own entity; / the dharma body is the body of the victors. / The dharma-nature permanently abides like space; / if demonstrated, the purification of phenomena will be obtained." Thus it is stated. Also from the Sarvabuddhaviṣayāvatārajñānālokālaṃkāra: "There is no moving from the nature of phenomena. Not moving from the nature of phenomena is to obtain the nature of phenomena. Obtaining the nature of phenomena is not conceptually proliferating even slightly. Why is that? Because of being generated by causes and conditions. Whatever is generated by causes and conditions is entirely unborn. Whatever is entirely unborn is definitely obtained. Whatever is definitely obtained does not abide together with mentally engaging in all phenomena." Thus it occurs, and so forth is stated. For that very reason, from the presence of Noble Nāgārjuna as well: "Whatever arises dependently, / that is not born by nature. / Whatever is not born by nature, / how can it be called 'born'? / Whoever conceptualizes birth / even for an entirely subtle entity, / that unwise one / has not seen the meaning of what arises from conditions." Thus it is stated. In another enumeration as well, the characteristics of the dependent nature, which is the conceptualized form, empty of and separated from the characteristics of the grasped and the grasper, the essence of the imputed [nature], at permanent times and at everlasting times, is the form of dharma-nature, the completely established characteristic. Regarding both the dependent and the completely established, what occurs as "emptiness is form" demonstrates this as very same. Because of definitely apprehending the essence of the completely established emptiness and the characteristic of the dependent form as very same, the very words "emptiness is form" are stated. Having thus expressed it by way of establishing the sameness, by way of refutation as well, what occurs as "emptiness is not other than form" and so forth refutes difference. Moreover, because it is completely established unchangingly and unmistakenly, it is the completely established; because it is not made by the potency of causes and conditions having assembled, it is unconditioned. The two other than that are unestablished. Because the continuum is not severed, it is permanent; that is the dharma body of the great Sage, existing ultimately. That very one exclusively is the ultimate truth; it is thus: it is called "nirvāṇa with an unbewildered nature." It is renowned that they say, "If it were not so, all efforts would become meaningless." To that, the explanation is: Depending on what is asserted as the characteristic of the dharma body as demonstrated, what is it that is called "existing ultimately"? Is it permanent like the self and so forth imputed by the tirthikas, or is it momentary? If it is the first alternative, what difference is there between this and the tenets of the tirthikas, the propounders of the limits of the Vedas, and so forth? From the Samādhirāja as well: "All phenomena are permanently empty of own-essence; / the sons of the victors thoroughly destroy entities. / All existences are primordially empty; / the emptiness of the partial ones is by the tirthikas." How is this as well to be realized? How can faults be imputed upon the tirthikas as well? But if according to the second alternative, then the earlier moment is the cause of the later moment, and for that as well, the faults of being at the same time and being at different times have already been demonstrated previously, so they will not be expressed again. Furthermore, what is stated as "that exclusively is the ultimate truth" and so forth is an intentional teaching to lead those who manifestly desire nirvāṇa, thus from the Akṣayamati: "If the conditioned does not exist, by stopping what would nirvāṇa become true?" By this, all of those are eliminated. From the Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikāprajñāpāramitā and so forth as well: "A perfectly complete buddha as well is like a magical illusion, like a dream. Nirvāṇa as well is like a magical illusion, like a dream. If there were some phenomenon superior even to nirvāṇa, I would say that it as well is like a magical illusion, like a dream." Thus it is stated. That efforts become meaningful will be demonstrated below. If one asks, "If one does not assert that the nondual exalted wisdom separated from the grasped and the grasper exists ultimately, how does it not become non-existent?" It does not become so, because dependently originated arising is asserted conventionally, and also ultimately it exists as passing beyond existence and non-existence. Reversing from very existence, apprehending it as very non-existence is thus the extreme of nihilism, the view of non-existence, [thinking]: "There is no father. There is no mother. This world does not exist. The next world does not exist. There is no fully ripened result of good and bad actions." If one is sinful with such an aspect characterized by deprecating the truths, the Three Jewels, and so forth, how could there be an occasion for viewing the exceedingly pure system of the middle way? From the Ratnāvalī as well: "Saying 'Water from a mirage,' / if one goes there with the mind of water, / then apprehending 'That water does not exist' / is very bewilderment." Thus it is stated, and "For one who asserts non-existence, / there is no mind because of being the support for awakening, / no assertion, and no conduct; / how could that one know non-existence?" By this as well, an answer is given. If one asks, "Because very existence is refuted, does it become very non-existence?" Because very non-existence is refuted, why would it not also become very existence? As for asserting that nondual consciousness exists ultimately: if the consciousnesses of those engaging in diverse causes are apprehended, how could there be nonduality? But if one does not apprehend what exists ultimately, how is it omniscience? If one says, "For you as well, this fault will be the same," it will not be, for the very non-seeing of ultimate existence is the only seeing, and not seeing a mirage as water does not entail ignorance. Thus it is stated, "The very not seeing of form is the very seeing of form," and so forth, and from the Samādhirāja as well: "Not seeing anything is the seeing of all phenomena." Thus it is stated. ^7-6

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-7]]

Similarly, like form, the aggregates from feeling up to consciousness at the end are in short shown to be empty of intrinsic nature. Therefore, one should understand it like this: feeling is empty. Emptiness is feeling. Emptiness is not other than feeling. Feeling is not other than emptiness. One should also apply [this] similarly to perception and so forth. ^7-7

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-8]]

After showing that the aggregates are empty of intrinsic nature, in order to show [that this] also [applies] to the sense-sources and elements, [the sūtra] states: "Śāriputra, thus..." and so forth. "Thus..." means like the aggregates. "All phenomena" refers to the sense-sources and elements; [this] is to be applied to the occurrence of the phrase "they are empty of intrinsic nature." ^7-8

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-9]]

After teaching the aspect of observing emptiness of intrinsic nature, in order to teach the aspect of observing signlessness, [the sūtra] states: "without defining characteristics." A defining characteristic is that by which [something] is defined or that which defines it. For example, the general defining characteristic of the aggregate of form is being form because it is susceptible to being broken. The general defining characteristic of the aggregate of feeling is feeling because it experiences. If one asks what difference there is regarding intrinsic nature, there is the difference made by specific and general defining characteristics. A specific defining characteristic is an intrinsic nature, which is the uncommon nature of blue and so forth. Since all phenomena that are aggregates, elements, and sense-sources lack those general defining characteristics, they are "without defining characteristics." Since [their] intrinsic nature is negated, being without defining characteristics and so forth are also established without the need for exertion by [their] nature. ^7-9

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-10]]

If one asks: "If all phenomena are empty and without defining characteristics, how is it that they arise from their respective conditions as they are, and cease due to the cessation of their respective conditions as they are?", it is in order to teach that the arising of the links of existence, such as "with ignorance as condition, formations [arise]; with formations as condition, consciousness [arises]," and the cessation of the links of existence, such as "with the cessation of ignorance, formations cease; with the cessation of formations, consciousness ceases," do not exist in this prajñāpāramitā, that [the sūtra] states: "unproduced, unceased." ^7-10

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-11]]

If one asks, "If that is so, how are these four noble truths of complete affliction and complete purification, along with their causes and effects, appropriate?", [the sūtra] states: "stainless, not without stain." Because they lack the intrinsic nature of the stains of affliction, all phenomena are "stainless." ^7-11

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-12]]

Because there is no complete purification by way of removing stains, it is stated that they are naturally free from stains, because all phenomena are naturally luminous. ^7-12

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-13]]

If one asks, "If that is so, how is it that on the Immovable ground, because the faults of marks have been abandoned, one abides spontaneously without marks, and thus faults are diminished; and how are the afflictions of the evil destinies and complete bewilderment and so forth abandoned, as is appropriate from before?", [the sūtra] states: "undiminished." Here, [this] should be viewed in terms of faults. ^7-13

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-14]]

Similarly, in order to teach that there is no increasing of qualities due to obtaining correct specific knowledges, great superknowledges, and unobstructed exalted wisdom respectively on the three [grounds] beginning with the ninth ground, [the sūtra] states: "unincreased." Here too, [the phrase] "by qualities" should be drawn out [implicitly], because it is stated thus in the Ratnakaraṇḍakasūtra as well. If one asks, "Why are exactly eight aspects stated, and why is the sequence stated like this?", what is explained here is the heart of the prajñāpāramitā, and the heart is supreme and principal among all other branches. The principal meaning of the prajñāpāramitā is the three doors of liberation, emptiness and so forth. That is subsumed by those eight aspects, and those very aspects are the sequence here. Thus, by the two, emptiness and signlessness, there is the meditative concentration of emptiness. By the middle four, because the four of arising, cessation, complete affliction, and complete purification are negated, there is the meditative concentration of signlessness. By the final two, there is the meditative concentration of wishlessness; for here one wishes to abandon faults or to obtain qualities, and because both of these are negated, there is wishlessness itself. ^7-14

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-15]]

Now, in order to teach the result of observing through the aspects that have been taught, [the sūtra] states: "Therefore, at that time..." and so forth. "Therefore" means because of observing through the aspects that have been taught. "At that time" means at that period. Regarding perfectly perceiving emptiness, it is applied to the occurrence "form is not to be perfectly perceived"; the very non-seeing of all phenomena is seeing emptiness. The occurrences of "no form" and so forth teach these negations of the aggregates, sense-sources, and elements. Regarding that, from the occurrence of "no form" up to the final occurrence of "no consciousness", the aggregates are negated. Beginning from the occurrence of "no eye", by this occurrence of "no phenomena", the sense-sources are negated. ^7-15

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-16]]

By the word "up to" following "no eye element", the fifteen—the form element, the eye consciousness element, and so forth—are taught implicitly. ^7-16

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-17]]

Because they are the final ones, [it mentions] the mind element and the mind consciousness element. Thus, to "without defining characteristics", "unproduced", and "unceased" and so forth as well, "no form" and so forth should all be applied. ^7-17

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-18]]

If one asks, "If the aggregates, elements, and sense-sources do not exist in emptiness, signlessness, and so forth, then how is it posited that formations arise with ignorance as condition, and similarly the twelve links arise from their respective conditions as they are, and are also ceased by the cessation of conditions?", it is true; they do indeed exist conventionally, but because they do not in emptiness, without defining characteristics, and so forth, [the sūtra] states: "no ignorance, no extinction of ignorance." By the word "up to", formations and so forth are taught implicitly. [It states:] "no aging and death." Because they do not exist, there is no extinction of them. ^7-18

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-19]]

If one asks, "If ultimately the links of existence and their extinction do not exist, then how do the noble ones see suffering and so forth as truths? What the noble ones see as truths is not appropriate to be false. If they are truths, how is it that the links of existence and their extinction do not exist?", therefore, regarding emptiness of intrinsic nature and so forth, [the sūtra] states: "suffering and..." and so forth. When the intrinsic nature of suffering does not exist because it is unproduced, at that time how could its cause, origin, exist? Therefore, there is also nothing whatsoever called the cessation of it, nor a path that becomes the method for its cessation. ^7-19

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-20]]

In order to teach that, because the faults of the nature of signs have been abandoned, even exalted wisdom is not ultimately established as an intrinsic nature, [the sūtra] states: "no exalted wisdom." In some manuscripts, the occurrence "no non-knowing" also appears; its meaning is this: If one thinks, "If, because emptiness is free from the faults of the complete affliction of signs, there is no exalted wisdom, then non-knowing, the defining characteristic of delusion, does exist," [the sūtra] states: "no non-knowing." How could this non-knowing possibly exist in emptiness of intrinsic nature? ^7-20

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-21]]

Similarly, in order to teach that if correct specific knowledges, powers, fearlessnesses, and other qualities, as well as the intrinsic nature that obtains them, do not exist, what is called "attainment" is not tenable, [the sūtra] states: "no attainment." ^7-21

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-22]]

Having thus negated the conceptualization of attainment, in order to negate its opposite, the conceptualization of non-attainment, [the sūtra] states: "no non-attainment." By this, one should understand that the profound meaning, transcending exalted wisdom and non-knowing, attainment and non-attainment, and free from the extremes of superimposition and deprecation, is taught. ^7-22

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-23]]

In that way, through the aspects taught above, because there is no attainment of any phenomena whatsoever in the prajñāpāramitā like this, therefore ^7-23

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-24]]

whatever intention, intense attachment, and thought for the sake of awakening there are, relying and completely relying upon the prajñāpāramitā—which is thoroughly endowed with the path of cultivating the defining characteristics exactly as they have been taught—and engaging with distinguished joyous effort, ^7-24

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-25]]

they abide. In awakening as well, no phenomenon whatsoever is thoroughly established. Accordingly, it is stated in the Candrapradīpasamādhi and so forth, when the Bhagavān taught the intrinsic nature of awakening: "Even extremely subtle phenomena do not exist; nor does what is called 'extremely subtle' exist." And so forth. In the Mahāvairocanābhisaṃbodhi, too, it is stated: "Master of Secrets, even extremely subtle phenomena do not exist, and are not to be apprehended; therefore, it is called unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening." If one thinks thus: "If there are no phenomena whatsoever in the prajñāpāramitā and unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening, then having cleared away phenomena that have the defining characteristics of faults, there is nothing whatsoever that becomes exalted wisdom either. And if there is also no attainment of desired qualities with defining characteristics, why do they abide relying on the prajñāpāramitā with reference to awakening?"—in so far as some precede [their actions] with hope, they engage in something; they all engage in order to abandon undesired faults and to obtain desired qualities. ^7-25

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-26]]

If, because everything is empty of intrinsic nature in every way, no phenomena whatsoever are apprehended, who engages in it? For what reason do they engage, and what is the result of engaging? If one asks this, [the sūtra] states: "because there are no obscurations of mind" and so forth. Because they hinder the thorough knowing of the genuine meaning just as it is, they obscure the mind; because they completely cover it, they are "obscurations of mind." Those [obscurations], moreover, are the twenty-two intense delusions—such as manifest adherence to persons and phenomena—and their eleven discordant classes of unwieldiness. In order to abandon those, in the sense of destroying them, their antidotes are sequentially the eleven grounds, the Extremely Joyous and so forth. As it is stated from the *Bhūmyavatāra*: "Through the perfection of eleven branches, / there are the eleven grounds, Extremely Joyous and so forth. / Two delusions and one unwieldiness [constitute] / the discordant class for each individually." Similarly, in the *Saṃdhinirmocana* as well, it is stated: "Regarding that, for the first ground, the intense delusion of manifest adherence to persons and phenomena, the intense delusion of the afflictions of the lower realms, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the second, the intense delusion of the error of subtle downfalls, the intense delusion regarding the various aspects of the transmigrations of karma, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the third, the intense delusion of desire of the desire realm, the intense delusion regarding the thorough perfection of the retention of what is heard, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the fourth, the intense delusion of craving for meditative absorptions, the intense delusion of craving for phenomena, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the fifth, the intense delusion of paying attention to completely turning away from and turning toward saṃsāra, the intense delusion of paying attention to turning away from and turning toward nirvāṇa, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the sixth, the intense delusion regarding the actualization of the occurrence of conditioned phenomena, the intense delusion of the abundant occurrence of signs, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the seventh, the intense delusion of the occurrence of subtle signs, the intense delusion regarding the method of paying attention to the complete absence of signs, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the eighth, the intense delusion of striving for the absence of signs, the intense delusion regarding mastery over signs, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the ninth, the intense delusion of mastery of retention with respect to immeasurable teachings of Dharma, immeasurable words and letters of Dharma, and higher and higher prajñā and eloquence, the intense delusion of mastery over eloquence, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the tenth, the intense delusion of great superknowledges, the intense delusion regarding engagement with subtle secrets, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class. For the ground of a Buddha, the intense delusion of extremely subtle attachment to all knowable objects, the intense delusion of obstruction, and their unwieldiness are the discordant class." Thus it is stated. Because those are absent, for those abiding in the prajñāpāramitā there is "no fear," meaning they are not afraid. ^7-26

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-27]]

It is taught that for those abiding in anything other than the prajñāpāramitā, there is fear from error. This is taught as follows: "Erroneous mind is engagement." Accordingly, it is taught in the Pramāṇavārttika: "In order to abandon erroneous superimpositions, / although there is no liberator, one exerts oneself." Where there is no error, there is no engagement. Accordingly, the Daśabhūmika teaches that when one attains patience with non-arising phenomena on the eighth ground, it is like the example of a person who dreams of being carried away by water and upon waking, the great effort to be freed from the water is reversed, and that very thing is their nirvāṇa, and so forth. Here as well, abiding in the prajñāpāramitā passes beyond error, which is intrinsic delusion, just as it is; "completely going" is to pass beyond error. ^7-27

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-28]]

Nirvāṇa is the cessation of conceptualizations. "Attained the end" is from reaching the limit. As it is stated in the Daśabhūmika: "Because the conceptualizations abandoned by meditation are completely abandoned, one attains the end of complete purity, because beyond that there are no conceptualizations to be abandoned." Thus it is stated. Because it attains the end, or because this nirvāṇa is the nature of the mind attaining the end, it is spoken of in that way. Because it is both passing beyond error and attaining the end of nirvāṇa, it is "passing beyond error and attaining the end of nirvāṇa"; because they will rely and abide on the prajñāpāramitā, bodhisattvas engage in it. By this greatness of abandonment, the cause for engaging for the sake of that is taught, because they engage for the sake of that. ^7-28

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-29]]

By what occurs as "in the three times" and so forth, because they become supreme among all sentient beings, they are taught for the sake of both the greatness of mind and the greatness of realization. "The three times" are the past, the future, and the present. "All Buddhas" who fully reside in them: they are "Buddhas" because they possess extraordinary intellect. Since they are both all of them and Buddhas, they are "all Buddhas." As it is stated from the Mahāvairocanābhisaṃbodhi: "Having thoroughly accomplished the ten grounds, / the aspect of mastery is obtained. / Whoever thereby knows all / empty, illusion-like phenomena here, / and knows the aspects of all sentient beings, / is called a Buddha." Thus it is stated. What occurs as "by the prajñāpāramitā" refers to the nature of the truth of the origin of all the exalted wisdoms of the Tathāgatas; it refers to whatever is called omniscience. What occurs as "relying on the prajñāpāramitā" is because this precedes perfect and complete awakening in time. This omniscience is to be seen as the uninterrupted samādhi; as it is stated from the Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā and its pith instruction, the Abhisamayālaṃkāra: "Uninterrupted in attaining Buddhahood, / it is the uninterrupted samādhi; / it is omniscience. / Its object of observation is without entity; / mindfulness is asserted as its dominant condition; / this aspect is peace itself; / signlessness is its defining characteristic." Thus it is stated. ^7-29

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-30]]

What occurs as "unsurpassed" is because there is nothing higher than this aspect of attainment, observation, and accomplishment. What occurs as "perfect" is unmistakably. What occurs as "complete" is because they share a single defining characteristic through signlessness itself. "Awakening" is realization. "Manifestly" is directly. "Completely awakened" is fully awakened. Accordingly, it is stated in the Pañcaviṃśatisāhasrikā Prajñāpāramitā: "Bhagavān, how is it unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening?" The Bhagavān replied: "Subhūti, just as the suchness of all phenomena is, so too is unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening." Subhūti asked: "Bhagavān, how is the suchness of all phenomena unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening?" "Subhūti, just as the suchness from form up to the suchness of nirvāṇa is, so too is unsurpassed, perfect, and complete awakening." Thus it is stated. Here, by the word "unsurpassed," because it becomes supreme among all sentient beings, it is the greatness of mind. The remainder of it is the greatness of realization. Having kept it in mind previously during the state of a bodhisattva, because one engages in the prajñāpāramitā for the sake of those three aspects of greatness, it is expressed as "for the sake of the three." At the time of completion, the former teaches the perfection of the result of abandonment, and the latter two teach the perfection of the result of realization; just as a king performs a sacrifice for the sake of a certain province, and ultimately obtaining the province is the result, similarly, relying on the prajñāpāramitā, it is because one becomes free from error and because one attains realization. ^7-30

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-31]]

In order to teach that the prajñāpāramitā itself is a mantra, "Therefore, Śāriputra," and so forth is stated. The defining characteristic of the prajñāpāramitā has already been taught. A mantra is because it is knowing and because it is protecting. Those very two meanings are clarified by the subsequent words. ^7-31

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-32]]

"Great" is because it possesses pervasion. Furthermore, pervasion is pervasion in time and pervasion in object; thus, this engages in all phenomena and all times without turning back. By this, it is taught to be distinct from the mantras made by Īśvara and others. As it is stated from the Mahāvairocanābhisaṃbodhi: "Completely passed beyond the three times, / existence arises in dependence. / Being seen and unseen results, / it exists in mind, speech, and body. / The result is a number of one eon; / it is explained to be of the worlds. / The result of mantra passes beyond / eons, it is explained by the perfect Buddhas. / The samādhi of the great sages, the Buddhas, / and the sons of the Victorious Ones, / abandons signs and is pure. / Accompanied by the signs of the worlds, / ripening is also ripened here; / by karmas the result is obtained. / At whatever time accomplishment is obtained, / at that time karmas are reversed, / because the mind lacks its own entity, / and because results and causes are abandoned. / Even from the arising of karma, / arising is like space." Thus it is stated. By this, because it is known and understood, or because desired accomplishment is obtained, it is "knowledge." Because it is both knowledge and great, it is "great knowledge." Because great knowledge itself is a mantra, it is the "mantra of great knowledge." ^7-32

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-33]]

Therefore, because there is no other highest, most supreme one apart from this, it is the "unsurpassed mantra." ^7-33

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-34]]

Because there is nothing equal or similar to this, the "unequaled" is space. Because it is equal to that, it is "equal to the unequaled." From the Bodhisattvapiṭaka, the words "equal to the unequaled" were spoken by the Bhagavān himself. From the Tathāgatācintyaguhyanirdeśa also, "However much some example / is applied to the qualities of a Buddha, / that is not in accordance with a Buddha; / that is a deprecation of the Victorious One. / Whatever is equal to the qualities of a Buddha, / there is one example for that, / it is taught as the realm of space, / of which there is no measure whatsoever," and so forth is stated. By this "equal to space," one should view pervasion, unhinderedness, unceasingness, and so forth. By those, the meaning of knowing is taught. ^7-34

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-35]]

In order to teach the meaning of protecting, "all suffering," and so forth is stated; pacifying and cutting off the continuum of all sufferings of defining characteristics, such as the suffering of conditioning, the suffering of change, and the suffering of suffering itself, it is the "mantra that completely pacifies all suffering." ^7-35

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-36]]

Having thus taught the meaning of the definitive word "mantra," in order to teach the meaning of the definitive word of that which is to be spoken secretly, "Because it is true, not false," and so forth is stated. "True" is suchness, because it is exactly as it is perfectly. Whatever is exactly as it is perfectly is unmistakableness itself. Therefore, it is appropriate as the mantra of the prajñāpāramitā. By this, this is taught: just as it is taught secretly to persons of dull faculties who are unable to view aspects when they are taught, they too should apprehend the prajñāpāramitā just like a mantra. ^7-36

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-37]]

The definitive word that "mantra" is "to be explained secretly" is to be considered based on the verbal root of this language. Alternatively, because abiding in the mind protects, it is a mantra. This means that the prajñāpāramitā is to be kept in mind respectfully and unceasingly. Because it is reasonable since it is logically justifiable, what is to be known should be firmly apprehended. ^7-37

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-38]]

What occurs as "Thus" is that one should train in the manner of the aspects that were spoken. ^7-38

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-39]]

What occurs as "Then" is without interruption at this occasion. What occurs as "from that samādhi" is from what was taught previously. What occurs as "having arisen" is having let go with the mind. ^7-39

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-40]]

Giving "Excellent" to the teacher and also acting in accordance with what was explained, ^7-40

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-41]]

the nature of giving "Excellent" is what occurs as "Excellent, excellent, son of noble family"; in that regard, repeating it twice is for the meaning of joy or because of great joy. ^7-41

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-42]]

By what occurs as "It is so" and so forth, he acts in accordance with what was explained. ^7-42

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-43]]

It is just so, and not otherwise. ^7-43

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^7-44]]

If one asks why that is, "One should train in the prajñāpāramitā," and so forth is stated. If one asks what it is like, "Just as you have taught" is stated; the meaning is, "One should train without contradicting what you have taught." ^7-44

## Rejoicing ^8-0

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-1]]

Thus, after the Bhagavān demonstrated his own accordance with the teaching by noble Avalokiteśvara, in order to teach, "Other Tathāgatas also deeply rejoice in whatever this teaching of yours is," "the Tathāgatas also rejoice" is stated. What is called "rejoice" ascertains the meaning of what must indubitably be done; the Tathāgatas indubitably rejoice in an explanation like this. By these two, accordance and rejoicing, it is also taught that it is fitting for this Tathāgata to deeply rejoice in the explanation. ^8-1

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-2]]

Because of that very fact, "The Bhagavān having spoken thus" is stated. What occurs as "thus" starts from Śāriputra's question up to "the Tathāgatas also rejoice." What occurs as "having spoken" is having been spoken by the Bhagavān. Although the noble Avalokiteśvara acts as the explainer here, nevertheless it is the word of the Tathāgata himself; for, since whatever is explained by śrāvakas and so forth, having been blessed by the Tathāgata like this, is entirely the word of the Tathāgata, what need is there to mention what is explained by the noble Avalokiteśvara, Samantabhadra, Mañjuśrī, and so forth, who have genuinely entered into buddhahood itself? The meaning of this I have already taught previously. What occurs as "rejoiced" is so called with respect to rejoicing and being glad by means of whichever hearing and explaining in particular, as appropriate, of the meaning of the heart of the prajñāpāramitā, which has become the supreme principal itself among the meanings to be expressed in the Śatasāhasrikā. ^8-2

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-3]]

Moreover, that is connected to being Śāriputra, the noble Avalokiteśvara, and so forth. By some, it is also connected to the Bhagavān; at that time, one should view the grasping of the prajñāpāramitā by those to be tamed, exactly as it is viewed as the principal of the meaning, as the cause of rejoicing. "And" is stated in order to include everything. By this, rejoicing and manifestly praising should also be included. Since bodhisattvas, bhikṣus, bhikṣuṇīs, upāsakas, and upāsikās are all seated here, they are called "the whole retinue." The gods are those of the class of the Four Great Kings and so forth. Humans are just well-known. As for asuras, it is because they abandoned the liquor that arose from churning the milky ocean. Furthermore, they are the sons of Diti and the sons of Danu. Gandharvas are the singers of the gods, namely Dhṛtarāṣṭra and so forth. Because they are seated together with gods and so forth, it is "the world with its gods, humans, asuras, and gandharvas." Because the gods are principal, they are stated first. Because it perishes, it is a "world"; it is the nature of the five aggregates. ^8-3

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-4]]

What the Bhagavān stated is the very nature of the explanation. "Manifestly praised" has the meaning of having apprehended it possessing manifest joy and possessing joy. ^8-4

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-5]]

Whatever merit I have obtained by analyzing the essence of the prajñāpāramitā, by that may the worlds become masters of the prajñāpāramitā. In the presence of the saṅgha of bhikṣus dwelling in the Brahmasambhava vihāra, this extensive explanation of the heart of the prajñāpāramitā was composed by Vimalamitra. ^8-5

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-6]]

The extensive explanation of the noble Heart of the Prajñāpāramitā composed by the Indian preceptor Vimalamitra is completed. ^8-6

![[bo-vimalamitra-tika#^8-7]]

It was translated, revised, and finalized by that very Indian preceptor Vimalamitra and the great Tibetan revising translators, Cib Bande Namkha, Yeshe Nyingpo, and others. ^8-7
